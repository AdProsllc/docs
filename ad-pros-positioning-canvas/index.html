1 Component One

Competitive alternatives

If Ad Pros didn't exist, what would the 8-figure operator do instead?

The biggest mistake here is naming other agencies first. Other agencies are an alternative — but they aren't the alternative we lose to most often. The alternative we actually lose to is the operator deciding to keep doing what they're already doing. The status quo is always the largest competitor in B2B services.

Five clusters of alternatives, ranked by how often they actually beat us in deals:

Cluster 1 — In-house team (the biggest competitor)

"We'll just hire a senior media buyer." The most common alternative. Operator has scaled past $10M, feels they've outgrown agencies, and decides to build internal capability. Hires one or two senior buyers, maybe a creative lead. Saves on retainer, gains control, loses agency talent depth and creative throughput.
How we beat it Senior buyer salary + benefits + tools + creative production cost ≈ $35-50K/month all-in for a fraction of the throughput. Ad Pros' fee structure scales with results; a salary doesn't. The in-house buyer is one person; we're a team with infrastructure.

Cluster 2 — Generic performance agency

"We'll go with a 'top-rated' agency on flat retainer." Operator hires another performance agency on $15-30K/month flat retainer. Most agencies look identical from the outside — same case studies, same channels, same promises. The operator picks based on referral, sales chemistry, or price. Six months later they're shopping again.
How we beat it Flat retainer is structurally indifferent to outcomes. Our performance kicker means our fee moves with their CPA. The other agency promises ROAS; we put fees on the line behind it. The structural difference is felt in negotiation, not described in pitches.

Cluster 3 — Fractional CMO / freelance media buyer

"We'll bring on a fractional or freelancer for 20 hrs/week." Cheaper than agency, more strategic than a junior hire. Common at $5-10M brands trying to act bigger. Works fine for a quarter or two, then the freelancer hits capacity, takes another client, or leaves. The brand is back to the same problem.
How we beat it A fractional is one brain on a clock. Ad Pros is a team with redundancy, infrastructure, creative output, and continuity. The fractional can't run an 8-channel test program; we can. The fractional can't anchor a Creator Network; we can.

Cluster 4 — Do nothing / current setup

"We'll just keep doing what we're doing." Underrated alternative. Operator is mediocrely happy with current results. Numbers are okay. Switching feels risky. The pain isn't acute enough to overcome inertia. They'll revisit "in 6 months."
How we beat it Quantify the cost of standing still. If their CPA is $300 and best-in-class is $180, the gap is $120 × monthly conversions × 12 months = a number large enough to make inertia look expensive. Don't sell against another agency; sell against the math of doing nothing.

Cluster 5 — Tier-1 holding-company agency

"We'll graduate to a 'real' agency now that we're at scale." Less common but high-stakes. Operator at $30M+ revenue gets recruited by a tier-1 agency promising integrated media, prestige, and bigger thinking. Ad Pros risks losing them on perceived brand authority, not capability.
How we beat it Tier-1 agencies pitch on prestige; we pitch on alignment. Holding companies bill hours; we bet on outcomes. The operator who graduates upward is choosing comfort and slower iteration; the operator who stays with us is choosing performance economics.
Working insight

The two alternatives we lose to most are in-house team and do nothing. The Ad Pros sales motion should systematically arm reps to defeat these two before pitching against any other agency. The math arguments — fully-loaded internal cost, cost of inaction — are more important than the case studies.

2 Component Two

Differentiated capabilities

What does Ad Pros have that the alternatives — in-house teams, generic agencies, fractionals, holdcos — do not?

Capabilities are not features. They are real, observable things Ad Pros can do that the listed alternatives structurally cannot. Stay on what we do, not what it means for the buyer (that's value, the next component).

Five capabilities, mapped against which alternatives they beat:

CapabilityBeats which alternative
Performance-linked fee structure — base retainer + variable fee scaled by CPA performance multiplier (the kicker model documented at docs.adpros.com/performance-pricing-model-v2) Generic agency (flat retainer, indifferent to outcomes), holdco (hours-billed), fractional (rate × time)
Creator Performance Network — $5K retainer + 10% of ad spend on creator content, 7% kept, 3% to creators. Equity-like exposure for creators, performance creative pipeline for brands. All alternatives. No one else has aligned creator economics; flat per-asset fees are the norm.
AI ops infrastructure (Noor) — 8-agent ClickUp workflow, BigQuery + Supabase, Claude API automation, ~5,000 ads/day intelligence pipeline In-house team (no one builds this internally for one brand), generic agency (no AI infra), fractional (impossible at one-person scale), holdco (legacy systems)
Senior team density — strategy/QA, account leadership, creative production, studio recording, all in-house. ~26 operators serving DTC + ecommerce. Fractional (one person), generic agency (junior staffing), in-house (cost-prohibitive at this density)
11-channel media buying capability — Meta, Google, TikTok, AppLovin, plus integrated tracking + attribution In-house team (typically masters 1-2 channels), fractional (channel-limited), generic agency (often single-channel deep)
Honest pressure-test For each capability, ask: would a sharp competitor say "we have that too"? If yes, it's not differentiated — it's table stakes. The five above survive that test. "We're data-driven" or "we have great creatives" do not. Skip them.
3 Component Three

Differentiated value

What does each capability translate to in operator-relevant outcomes — outcomes a CFO would put on a slide?

Value is the capability translated into the language of P&L impact. The translation discipline matters: a feature like "performance-linked fees" is not value. The value is "your agency cost moves with your CPA, so you don't pay full price during a soft month." That's a sentence the operator can take to their board.

Capability → Value mapping

Performance-linked fee structure
Base retainer + variable fee scaled by CPA multiplier
Lower variance in agency cost during weak months. Predictable upside for the agency creates real alignment, which means real seniority on the account. The operator stops paying full freight when results dip — and the agency only earns the kicker when results justify it.
Creator Performance Network
$5K + 10% of creator-content spend; 7/3 split with creators
Continuous creative throughput at performance-aligned cost. Creators have skin in the game, so they iterate. The brand stops paying $5-10K per static asset and starts paying for creative that converts. New asset velocity rises 3-5×; CAC on creator-driven creative drops because the supply is built around what works.
AI ops infrastructure (Noor)
8-agent ClickUp pipeline, BigQuery + Supabase, ~5K ads/day intelligence
Decisions made on data the in-house team can't process at this scale. The intelligence layer surfaces what's working and what's mediocre across thousands of ads, so the operator's portfolio gets reallocated faster than competitors using dashboards or human review. Faster reallocation = compounding ROAS advantage.
Senior team density
~26 operators, in-house strategy/QA/creative/account
No "junior account manager" tax. The operator's account is run by people who could be running it in-house — but with the leverage of a full team behind them. Trust gets built fast, decisions get made fast, errors get caught before they ship.
11-channel media buying
Meta, Google, TikTok, AppLovin, plus full attribution stack
Channel diversification without channel dilution. As one channel saturates or platform changes hit (iOS, ad costs, algorithm shifts), the spend shifts to where ROAS is currently strongest — without hiring 11 specialists. The operator gets a hedged portfolio, not a single-channel bet.
The compound value claim

Lower variance in agency cost. Higher creative throughput. Faster reallocation. Senior decision-making. Channel-hedged spend. Together: a performance partnership that lowers CPA over time and stabilizes cost during downturns. That's not five claims — it's one operating model with five inputs.

4 Component Four

Best-fit customers

Who values these capabilities so much they'll pay performance-linked fees, refer their peers, and stay 3+ years?

"8-figure operator" is the headline. But Dunford's discipline says best-fit must be narrow enough to fit on a single page. The version below is what to actually use in outbound, partner conversations, and ICP filtering — the more specific the better.

Tier 1 — Core Best-Fit (the Superconsumer)
$10-50M annual revenue $300K+ monthly ad spend DTC ecommerce Founder-led / founder-CEO Has tried 2+ agencies Treats ad spend as P&L line Wants to scale beyond $50M Owns gross margin reporting

This is the operator who closes fastest, retains longest, and refers the most. They've been burned by flat-retainer agencies, they understand CPA mechanics intuitively, and they're sophisticated enough to reward alignment with loyalty. They are the case-study fountain — every story we publish should feature one of them.

Tier 2 — Adjacent Fit (high-fit upgrade path)
$5-10M revenue, scaling fast $150-300K monthly spend DTC with clear unit economics Ambitious founder, 2-3 yrs in Open to performance pricing

The aspirational tier. They want to think like an 8-figure operator before they're one. Closing them earlier and growing them inside the agency is a long-arc bet — but the ones who make it become Tier 1 Superconsumers in 18-24 months. Worth a calibrated number of these in the book of business.

Tier 3 — Acceptable Fit (selectively)
$50M+ revenue brands CFO/CMO buyer (not founder) More operationally complex

Larger brands where the buyer is no longer the founder. Higher-revenue accounts, but slower decisions, more procurement friction, and weaker alignment to performance pricing. Worth taking selectively when fit is strong; not the engine of the book.

Not-Fit (explicitly disqualify)
Sub-$5M revenue Sub-$100K monthly spend B2B SaaS / lead-gen primary Service businesses Brand-first, ROAS-skeptical Procurement-led buying

Naming who we're not for is as important as naming who we are for. These accounts close slowly, churn fast, eat senior time, and don't generate referrals into our actual ICP. Sales reps should be empowered to disqualify — not because the brand is bad, but because the fit isn't there.

5 Component Five

Market category

In which market category does Ad Pros' differentiated value pop most clearly to the best-fit operator?

Dunford is firm here: don't create a new category unless you're at $200M+ revenue with the budget to educate a whole market. We aren't. So the question is which existing category to position inside — and that choice changes everything downstream.

The four candidate categories

CategoryIf we pick thisVerdict
Performance marketing agency Get compared to Common Thread, Pilothouse, Disruptive. The default category. We win on partnership model and AI infra. Default — but generic.
Growth partner / Fractional CMO firm Get compared to Bonfire, GrowthBook, NoGood. Buyer expects strategy + execution. Pricing pressure rises. Wrong — we're not strategy-only.
Media buying firm Get compared to MuteSix, Tinuiti. Single-channel-deep, often executional. Commodity pricing dynamics. Wrong — too transactional.
Performance partner (the lane we're claiming) Almost no one credibly occupies it. Implies shared outcome, equity-like alignment, infrastructure-grade ops. We define the comparison set. Correct — uncontested at agency level.
The hybrid move We position publicly as "performance partner" (the lane Ad Pros claims), but we anchor the SEO and category-search context inside "performance marketing agency" so buyers searching the existing category find us. The line — "performance partner for 8-figure operators" — does both jobs at once. Publicly differentiated, search-discoverable.

Why this works: "performance partner" is not a new category — it's a sharper position inside the agency category. It borrows the credibility of the existing category (operators know what an agency is) while distancing us from the commodity pricing dynamics of "agency" alone. Nine words doing the work of an entire repositioning brief.

06 Synthesis

The positioning, in one breath.

All five components collapsed into a single working positioning statement. Use this internally for alignment, not externally as copy — the public version is the nine-word headline.

Internal Positioning Statement

Ad Pros is the performance partner for 8-figure DTC operators who treat ad spend as a P&L line.

For
Founder-led DTC ecommerce brands at $10-50M revenue and $300K+ monthly ad spend who have tried agencies before.
Who are
Frustrated by flat-retainer agencies that don't share outcomes, hesitant to build a full in-house team, and looking to scale beyond $50M without losing creative throughput or channel diversification.
Ad Pros is
A performance partner — not a vendor, not an agency in the traditional sense — that puts its fee on the line behind their CPA.
That
Combines performance-linked pricing, the Creator Performance Network, AI-grade ops infrastructure (Noor), senior team density, and 11-channel media capability into a single operating model.
Unlike
Generic performance agencies (flat fees, no skin in the game), in-house teams (high cost, low throughput), fractional CMOs (one brain on a clock), and tier-1 holdcos (prestige over alignment).
Our product
Lowers CPA variance during downturns, raises creative velocity during scaling, and compounds ROAS advantage through faster reallocation than any alternative can match.
