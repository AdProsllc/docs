Retention Engine

Why Creator Engine clients leave at three months — and the system that changes it

01 The model on one page

Forward this, not the document

The problem. Creator Engine clients average three months. 80% of the ones we lost were hitting their targets. Most cancelled without warning.

The cause. Not absence — we generate roughly 55 touchpoints per client life. The weekly call broadcasts Ad Pros' output across four functions and never asks about the client's business. There is no written definition of success, so the client evaluates us privately against a standard we have never seen. The first time that standard is spoken aloud is the cancellation.

The fix. Two artifacts, not forty. A signed Scoreboard with falsifiable day-30/60/90 numbers, and a Mutual Contract that names what the client owes us. Everything else is adopted from work that already exists.

Next three actions. Ask every active client what number their boss judges them on — only Nehal is in the room to ask. Write the Scoreboard template from CPN's own figures. Name what comes off Danna's list to make room.

02 The binding constraint

A dense surface that emits nothing

The constraint moved four times during the interrogation. It did not land where anyone expected.

The first hypothesis was that retention had no owner. True, but downstream — the seat is now named and the problem did not move with it. The second was that the relationship surface was too thin to instrument. That turned out to be wrong in the opposite direction.

What the weekly call actually is

The agenda is the org chart. Housekeeping, Performance Review, Next Steps, Creatives — four sections mapping onto the four people in the room, each presenting their function. It was never designed as a client conversation. It is a standup with the client watching.

The August 27 deck for one client runs 33 slides. Every one reports Ad Pros' work. The slide headed Core Issues / Questions lists fourteen items, all internal technical problems shown to the client: attribution and misattribution, a possible schedule-event CAPI issue, EMQ, ad dilution across ads, whether different messaging is producing wrong lead types.

22 concepts assigned and delivered until tomorrow EOD! The goal is still 12 concepts per week, but we are compensating for the first weeks that the engines were still heating up.

That is a slide defending our own throughput.

Absent from all 33 slides: their revenue. Whether the appointments closed. What a deal is worth to them. Whether their sales team can absorb 351 appointments in a week. Their margin. Their internal target. Anything they are worried about. The single client-business question in the deck — variance in appointment quality — is marked TBD.

The mechanism A client cannot express dissatisfaction in a meeting that never asks them a question. There is no agenda item where I'm not sure this is working for us fits. So they attend, reply, hit targets, nod through thirteen performance readouts — and then cancel, because the first honest conversation about value is the one where they end it.

The gap that produces it

The sale is made on the client's business economics — the CPN plan is explicit that clients run around 1.5x ROAS and are sold on new-customer and LTV logic, never on first-order profit. Every conversation afterwards is conducted in spend, ROAS and asset volume. We sell on their number and report on ours.

Compounding it: nothing is written down at the close. What a client sees at thirty days today is assets, campaigns live, initial results — three statements that cannot fail. Assets always exist. Campaigns are always live. Results are always initial. An unfalsifiable day-30 means neither side can ever detect that the relationship is off track.

The binding constraint

There is no falsifiable, mutually agreed definition of success — so the client evaluates us privately against a standard we have never seen, in a weekly meeting that broadcasts our output instead of asking about their business.

Amendment from the pressure-test: any definition that binds only Ad Pros makes the position worse, not better. The Scoreboard and the Mutual Contract are one artifact, not two.

Two findings that reshape scope

Speed is a relationship input, not a parallel system. Retention Engine does not own win rate or creative velocity — CPN does. It owns how slowness is experienced: whether the client knows what is coming and when, whether a delay is named before they notice it, and whose delay it was. A 16% win rate instead of 20% churns nobody. Nine days of silence does.

Volume is not the lever. 55 touchpoints, zero signal. Every instinct toward more contact should be resisted. The fix is stakes per touch and triggered contact, not frequency.

03 The first build

The Scoreboard

One page, signed before spend starts. It states what the client will see at day 30, 60 and 90 in numbers that can fail.

This is assembly, not invention. Every figure below already exists in the CPN model — 54 videos per client, a 20% win rate, 4% breakthrough, four days product-to-video, $5K attributed per video. They have simply never been pointed at the client as a promise.

Numbers are per client and set at signature against their category and roster depth — these are model defaults, not a universal promise. What matters is that each row can be checked and can fail.

The half that is not about us

Alongside the delivery numbers, the Scoreboard records the client's own number — the one their boss judges them on. Not spend, not ROAS, not asset volume. Their close rate on the appointments we generate. What a closed deal is worth. What their internal target was for the quarter. Whether their sales team can absorb the volume.

Only one person can collect this Discovery cannot start with Danna. She has no access to a client's close rates or margins. Nehal is the only person in the room who can ask, and asking it is the single highest-leverage act in this document. Danna owns chasing the signature, tracking completion and firing triggers — not the discovery.

Why it must be falsifiable

The first winner already lands around day 21–30 under the current model, well before the cancellation decision forms at week 6–8. Clients are seeing proof and leaving anyway. That is the same fact as 80% were hitting targets, arriving from a different direction — and it means the problem is not that proof arrives late. It is that nobody agreed in advance what proof would look like, so the client invents their own version privately and we never learn what it was.

What the evidence says

The strongest available data on why clients fire agencies comes from surveys that ask both sides the same question. Clients rank delivery and the agency didn't understand our business at the top; budget ranks near the bottom. Agencies believe the reverse — they rank budget cuts and leadership changes first. Budget is the polite exit.

04 The second build

The Mutual Contract

Agency retention literature assumes the client owes you nothing. Every framework treats the agency as the sole performer. That assumption does not survive a model with physical product and creator approvals.

The two genuinely new causes in the pre-mortem were products shipping slowly and slow approvals on creators and scripts. Both are client failures that produce our churn. At four days product-to-video inside a five-week ramp, a week of slow approvals is a 20% cut to the proof window.

What each side owes

Ad Pros commitsThe client commits
Videos live against the day-30/60/90 scheduleA named approver with a 48-hour window on scripts and creators
Winners produced at the agreed rateProduct shipped within 5 business days of creator approval
Learn spend deployed and reportedThe economic buyer attends the three checkpoint calls
Any slip named before the client notices itAccess to their close rate, deal value and internal target

The mechanism: the ramp clock pauses

When a client-side commitment slips, the day-30/60/90 dates move by the length of the delay. Not a penalty, not a confrontation — arithmetic both sides agreed to at signature.

This does the real work. It converts a one-way performance guarantee into a mutual commitment, which is the difference between a scoreboard that strengthens our position and one that becomes a rod for our own back. It also makes client-side delay visible at the moment it happens, rather than surfacing as disappointment at day 90.

The objection this answers

You're formalizing your own subordination. A signed scoreboard is a performance guarantee you volunteered, and it invites monthly referendum on numbers you don't fully control — Meta's algorithm, their sales team, their fulfilment.

That objection survives the pressure-test and is the reason these two artifacts ship together. A scoreboard alone hands the client a stick. A scoreboard with a mutual contract makes the engagement a joint operation with a shared clock.

On term length

Today, ninety days is described as the start, but clients leave inside it — which means there is no term at all. The arithmetic is unforgiving: a 13-week term with a 5-week ramp gives the client eight weeks of steady state to judge on, and their renewal decision covers a period that includes the weeks we told them would underperform. We are asking them to judge the marriage on the honeymoon and the hangover.

We are not fixing this with a longer contract. Close rate is the most sensitive input in the CPN model, and trading it for term length is the wrong trade at this stage. The 12-month frame gets revisited at 20+ clients. The interior structure of the 90 days is the free fix.

05 Operating rhythm

Fewer touches, higher stakes

The call, redesigned

Two changes, and the second matters more than the first.

Attendance, not cadence. Four people on every weekly call at fifteen clients is sixty person-hours a week — and Nehal is fifteen of them, alongside the three people who own win rate, production and creator supply. That is the CPN sprint's own delivery bench sitting in meetings.

ConfigurationPerson-hours/week at 15 clients
Weekly, four people — today60
Biweekly, four people30
Weekly, two people30
Biweekly, two people + written update in the off week15

One account owner plus one rotating specialist. The last row is the destination — but only after the conversation changes. Halving a call that emits no signal produces a call that emits no signal half as often.

The agenda opens with their number. Not spend, not ROAS, not asset volume. What happened in their business, whether the volume is absorbable, what their boss is asking. Our output moves to the back half, and to the written update.

What the written update carries

Weekly in the off-week, and it does the work the deck used to do badly: what shipped, what is queued, what slipped and whose slip it was, and the scoreboard position. Attempt the meeting twice; when it does not happen, the recap goes out anyway.

What we are deliberately not doing More volume, more depth, more impromptu calls, more internal calls, more work sessions. There are already 55 touchpoints producing zero signal; adding to that channel produces more nothing, across four people, at fifteen clients. Unscheduled contact is an output of a working system — evidence that something surfaced — not an input that can be scheduled.
06 Health and triggers

The layer that comes third

A health score reads deviation from a baseline. With five clients and no scoreboard, there is neither. This ships after the first two artifacts, not alongside them.

The case for waiting is empirical. Analysis of 15,000 accounts found roughly 45% of manually assigned health ratings contradicted what the underlying data showed — accounts rated healthy while engagement declined, accounts flagged at risk while deepening. The mechanism is copying a template instead of backtesting against real outcomes. At five clients there is nothing yet to backtest, and a score that looks rigorous while predicting nothing is worse than no score.

The index, when it ships

DimensionWeightWhat it reads
Relationship depth — structural40%Thread count, champion tenure, seniority of contact, new procurement or finance appearing, days since an economic-buyer conversation
Engagement and sentiment — behavioural25%Reply latency and attendance against that account's own trailing average, shaping the plan versus rubber-stamping, escalation language. Subjective input capped at 10 of the 25
Delivered value20%Position against the signed scoreboard — never a generic benchmark
Deliverables — throughput15%Videos live, winners, brief-to-live latency, client-side approval latency

The weighting is deliberate and it is a survivability index, not a performance index. Performance is already visible in Northbeam, Triple Whale and Pulse. This exists to measure what those cannot see.

Mechanics that matter more than the weights

  • Every signal scored against that account's own trailing baseline. A client who has always replied in eighteen hours is not disengaging at eighteen hours.
  • Trend over snapshot. Three consecutive months of decline outranks any absolute number.
  • Recency decay. A missed call last week outweighs one in March.
  • Only the sentiment slice is human-adjustable. Whoever owns an account will nudge it green; capping the subjective portion caps the damage.

Overrides — force red regardless of composite

With deliverables at 15%, the composite cannot detect a dead engine on its own. The override list is load-bearing, not a safety net.

  • Champion departure, or a new marketing or growth lead arriving
  • Contract, notice or pause questions raised
  • Zero videos launched in 30 days
  • A checkpoint missed with no intervention logged
  • Procurement or finance appearing where they were not before
  • Payment overdue past threshold

The objection we are choosing to carry

You're solving a symptom. Clients cancel because there is nothing distinctive to stay for — 54 videos a month is a volume promise, and volume promises get re-shopped the moment someone quotes cheaper. A scoreboard just lets them measure a commodity more precisely.

This survives and is not answerable in ninety days. CPN already stakes the differentiation on the brief-to-feedback loop. It stays in the register rather than changing this plan — but if churn holds above 15% after two quarters with scoreboards signed and checkpoints running, this is the explanation to reach for next.
