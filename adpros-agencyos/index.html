agencyOS — how Ad Pros runs itself

agencyOS runs EOS as its management framework and instruments it with Pulse. It defines valid outcomes from vision down to service level, logs all execution — human and AI — in one schema, continuously identifies the constraint limiting each outcome, and uses that record as the basis for performance management. EOS supplies the rituals: the Accountability Chart, quarterly Rocks, the Level 10 meeting, and IDS. Pulse supplies the two components EOS leaves thin — a Scorecard that fills itself, and a process registry that versions itself.

The stack — what sits on what

EOS is the management framework. agencyOS is EOS instrumented. growthOS is the sibling system clients buy. Pulse is the software layer both operating systems run on — not a third system.

The core inversion

Standard OKR software fails for two dominant reasons: it's set-and-forget, reviewed too rarely to matter, and accountability gets diffused across shared objectives. This system is built backward from those two failure modes. Outcomes are defined top-down (vision → mission → values → company → department → client → service), but the operating discipline runs bottom-up and daily: every OKR is surfaced in the meetings that already happen, waterfalled to that day's work, and every constraint gets exactly one owner the moment it's found.

Validity rule

An outcome only counts if it ladders, with a stated mechanism, to a company OKR. No mechanism, no tracking.

One schema, two actor types

Humans and AI agents write to the same activity log. Cost and quality are compared on equal footing.

Constraints, not dashboards

Nothing gets instrumented unless it serves a live constraint question. Every finding ends in a decision.

What people are measured on

In order: values compliance (veto), outcome attainment adjusted for what was actually controllable, growth in scope of outcomes owned, and — for senior roles — leverage created in others' results. Never raw activity volume, and never anything not system-logged. This is deliberate: it is the answer to the standard critique of intense measurement cultures, that people become guarded the moment they know they're being scored on everything they do.

agencyOS = EOS + Pulse

agencyOS is not a new management framework. It is EOS (Entrepreneurial Operating System) adopted as the human coordination layer, instrumented by Pulse for a company where a meaningful share of the workforce is AI agents. EOS is deliberately thin on measurement — it says "keep a weekly scorecard" and stops. At 60 clients, 2,100 creators and 1,200 videos per month, that scorecard cannot be typed in by hand. Everything built here fills the two components EOS leaves lightest: Data and Process.

Half-adopted EOS is worse than none — it works because of the discipline, not the templates. agencyOS assumes EOS is genuinely run, not borrowed as vocabulary for a custom system.

The Accountability Chart

Not an org chart. Seats first, names second — each seat has 5 roles and one accountable owner. One person may sit in several seats early on; the chart shows the company you are building, not the one you have.

GWC

A person keeps a seat only if they Get it, Want it, and have the Capacity to do it. All three, or the seat is wrong. In agencyOS this test extends to agent seats: capability, scope fit, and reliability.

Rocks

3–7 company priorities per 90 days, each with one owner. Anything not a Rock is not this quarter's work. This is the mechanism that stops the deep-work list from becoming an infinite queue.

The Level 10 Meeting — the weekly engine

Two thirds of the meeting is IDS. Every other segment is deliberately short — they exist to find the issues that IDS then solves.

Same day, same time, same agenda, 90 minutes, started and ended on time. Most of the meeting is spent solving, not reporting.

IDS in detail

Identify the real issue beneath the stated one. Discuss once — everyone says their piece a single time, no circling. Solve with a specific to-do and one owner. agencyOS logs the solve as a versioned process change so it cannot silently reappear.

The 3-Step Process Documenter

Identify your core processes, name them, then document only the 20% of steps that drive 80% of the result — a handful of bullets per process, not a manual. Then get it followed by all.

Meeting pulse

Weekly L10 at every level, quarterly sessions, annual planning. The rhythm is the product; skipping it is how EOS installations quietly die.

OKRs vs. Rocks — not two goal systems

This is the single most likely source of confusion, so it is stated explicitly. OKRs are the continuous measurement layer: they always exist, they are always instrumented, and Pulse computes them without anyone typing a number. Rocks are the 90-day subset the leadership team chooses to actively work on — 3 to 7, one owner each, set at Quarterly Pulsing.

Put plainly: every Rock must move a named OKR, but not every OKR has a Rock this quarter. An OKR without a Rock is being watched, not worked. If a watched OKR drifts off track, it surfaces on the Scorecard, becomes an Issue, and gets IDS'd — and if the fix is bigger than a to-do, it becomes next quarter's Rock. That is the whole relationship.

What this shows

The daily ring is the only one that touches every constraint the moment it appears. Everything outside it exists to catch what daily couldn't close: a constraint unresolved after a week escalates to the client review, one that survives two weeks escalates to department, and so on outward. Nothing skips a ring — that's what keeps a small blocker from becoming a quarterly surprise, and it's the direct antidote to the "set-and-forget" failure mode that kills most OKR programs.

Five rhythms — and where each comes from

The escalation path is the core mechanic: nothing is solved at the wrong altitude, and nothing that matters falls through.

Why the 13-Week Race matters here

The client survival OKR — time-to-$100K attributed under 90 days — is a 13-week race, one per client. Tracking it as a weekly trajectory rather than a deadline is the difference between intervening in week 4 and discovering the miss at day 90, which is exactly when churn happens.

"Stucks"

Thean's daily-huddle term for a blocker someone cannot clear alone. It is the same object as a constraint row in this system — named, owned, and dated. Saying "stuck" out loud daily is what keeps the constraint log honest.

Status before the meeting

Thean's rule is that statuses are entered the day before, so the meeting solves rather than reports. agencyOS goes one step further: nobody enters them at all. Pulse computes and colors them, and the meeting starts at the adjustment.

The constraint loop — DIKW
What this shows

The dashed return path is the entire point. Without it, DIKW is just a reporting chain that ends at a decision and stops. With it, every decision — a process version, an OKR revision, an explicit no-action — becomes the next cycle's brief, so the system's own outputs sharpen its own inputs. Constraint management isn't a separate process running alongside DIKW; the Information → Knowledge → Wisdom conversion is the constraint review, run at every OKR level.

This is IDS with a memory

DIKW and EOS's IDS are the same motion at different resolutions, and they are not competing systems. IDS is what a room does in a meeting: Identify the real issue, Discuss it once, Solve it with an owner and a to-do. DIKW is what the system does underneath, continuously and with a record.

Data (activity and results rows) becomes Information when a gap is localized to a specific OKR — that is Identify, done by query rather than by memory. Knowledge is the constraint named, categorized, and judged for controllability — that is Discuss, with evidence instead of opinion. Wisdom is the decision, which becomes a versioned process change and next cycle's input — that is Solve, made permanent. The one thing DIKW adds to IDS is that the solve is written into the process registry, so a solved issue cannot quietly return.

Where EOS lives in the schema

actors holds EOS seats — humans and agents alike — with the GWC judgment and the accountable owner for every agent seat. constraints is the EOS issues list, persisted: each row is an identified issue with one owner, a controllability judgment, and a required decision, so a solved issue leaves a record instead of a memory. okrs feeds the weekly Scorecard directly.

What this shows

okrs is the schema's spine — activity, results, and constraints all key off an okr_id, so nothing in the system can exist without laddering to a valid outcome. values_registry is the one static reference table: it doesn't get written to by activity, only pointed at when a constraint fires as a values violation. processes is the registry that matters most day to day — it's versioned, tagged by Wardley stage (genesis / custom / product / commodity), and is the actual automation roadmap once populated.

Ingestion pipeline
What this shows

The error path runs parallel to the dashboard path, not downstream of it — pipeline_runs and dq_issues are written at every stage, so a broken transform surfaces in Slack before it silently produces an empty or wrong dashboard. Silence detection matters more than failure detection: pipelines usually die quietly. The raw landing-zone layer is the single biggest anti-breakage decision — a source's schema change only ever breaks the transform, never the raw data itself, so a fix can always replay from the raw table.

What this shows

Same escalation logic as the review cadence, applied to the tooling itself. Agent failures get fixed at the agent loop first — a low-scoring trace becomes an eval case, and no new process version ships until it passes the growing eval suite. Only what the agent loop can't resolve becomes a process-level constraint finding; only what recurs across processes becomes a system-level fix. This is what keeps the tracker from calcifying — it's subject to its own constraint-identification discipline, not exempt from it.

One decision gates almost everything. The data pipeline is the only workstream that can run in parallel.

  • Accountability Chart, finalized — seats before names, five roles per seat, one owner each, agent seats included with their human owner named.

  • Values registry: which values, encoded as testable behaviors + violation definitions (this is the EOS People Analyzer, made queryable)
  • Quality rubric per process family (creative, outreach, media buying)
  • ClickUp → BigQuery sync method (webhook vs. scheduled pull)
  • The weekly Scorecard's 5–15 numbers, selected from the cascade — which OKRs earn a permanent line
Deferred — revisit after pilot
  • Challenge mechanism — a formal path to contest a constraint finding or attribution with evidence, borrowed from Bridgewater's dot-collector pattern. Mechanics unclear; park until the performance layer is live and there's a real dispute to design against.
  • Eval-gated agent releases — no agent version ships without passing evals generated from its own past failures. Actioning unclear until Langfuse traces accumulate enough volume to build a real eval suite from.
Outside perspectives used to pressure-test this design

Mik Kersten (Flow Framework) — insights should come from ground truth in the toolchain, not self-report; watch that OKRs don't get used to micromanage features. His four work-types split (feature / defect / risk / debt) is folded in as work_type on the activity log.

Eli Goldratt (Theory of Constraints) — only the bottleneck matters; measuring everything equally is waste. This is why the system is constraint-first, not dashboard-first.

Bridgewater (Dot Collector) — believability-weighted, evidence-based feedback works, but constant evaluation made people guarded and was psychologically costly for many. The system takes the believability-through-track-record idea and deliberately drops the live peer-rating mechanic.

Keith Rabois (barrels vs. ammunition) — execution capacity isn't the scarce resource once AI agents are ammunition; judgment and outcome ownership are. This is why the performance layer weights scope-of-ownership growth over volume.

W. Edwards Deming — most variation in results comes from the system, not the individual; per-person metrics used punitively will always degrade the data. This is the basis for "people are never evaluated on anything not system-logged."

Michael Gerber (E-Myth) — the process registry is worthless if it documents aspiration rather than operating reality. Every process in the registry must reflect what actually happens, not what the SOP says should happen.

Alex Hormozi — most of this is infrastructure people build to avoid a decision; the real question is which 3 numbers would double the business. Kept as a standing filter on every dashboard view.

Are we running EOS, or building our own system?

Running EOS. The Accountability Chart, V/TO, quarterly Rocks, Level 10 meeting, and IDS are adopted as written — they are solved problems with a shared vocabulary many hires already speak. agencyOS is what we add underneath: the Scorecard fills itself, the process registry versions itself, and agents can hold seats. We deviate in exactly one place — a daily constraint layer on top of the weekly L10.

What is the difference between agencyOS and growthOS?

agencyOS is internal: how Ad Pros runs itself. growthOS is external: the system a client buys, installed in their ad account. Pulse is the software layer both run on — not a third system. agencyOS's Scorecard aggregates growthOS outcomes across the whole client book.

Do Rocks replace OKRs?

No, and confusing them is the main failure risk. OKRs are the continuous measurement layer — always on, computed automatically. Rocks are the 3–7 priorities the leadership team chooses to actively work each quarter. Every Rock must move a named OKR; not every OKR gets a Rock. An OKR without a Rock is being watched, not worked — and if it drifts, it becomes an Issue, gets IDS'd, and may become next quarter's Rock.

Why is this a performance management tool and not just an ops dashboard?

Because it was explicitly designed to be one. That decision is what makes the outcome-attainment, scope-trajectory, and leverage metrics load-bearing rather than nice-to-have — and it's why values compliance is a veto, not a tiebreaker.

How do we avoid this becoming surveillance that people route around?

Three structural guards: nothing invisible can be used against someone (only system-logged facts count), values violations override attainment either way, and misses caused by a constraint outside someone's control are excluded from their record by design. The challenge mechanism (deferred) would add a fourth: a formal way to contest a finding.

What's the atomic unit of the whole system?

One row in activity_log — a single unit of execution, human or AI, tied to exactly one service-level OKR. Department, client, role, and pod are dimensions on that row, not separate trackers.

Why append-only?

It removes an entire class of bugs by construction — no race conditions, no silently overwritten history. "Current state" is just a view over the latest rows, and every past state stays queryable.

How do we handle fuzzy attribution — most results have several contributors?

The results table carries an explicit attribution_confidence field (high / medium / low). Fuzzy attribution is stated, never faked, and constraint reviews evaluate processes over batches rather than crediting individual rows.

What's the simplest version we could actually ship first?

Raw landing tables + a pipeline_runs log + one Slack alert, for one pod and one client, with Pulse agents writing directly since we control both ends. Expansion only happens after that pilot runs its weekly review four times without manual intervention.

What stops this from becoming stale infrastructure nobody reads?

The system loop: dashboard view usage is instrumented, and views nobody reads get deleted monthly. The tracker is subject to its own constraint-identification discipline, not exempt from it.
