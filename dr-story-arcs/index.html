    Direct Response Story Arcs

    Hook library

    Eighteen hook principles in five families. A hook is an attention mechanism, not an opening line: each family has one job inside an arc, so it shows up wherever that job is needed, not only at the top. Definitions are the mechanism; "use it" is how to write the beat.

    Making it about themJob: qualificationPeople attend to what is about them.
    Self-relevanceDescribe the viewer, not the topic. Name their situation, symptom, or moment so precisely that skipping feels like ignoring themselves.Use it: "If you [specific situation], this is for you." Filter out everyone else on purpose.
    Goal relevanceAttach the content to a goal they already hold. No persuasion needed if it serves what they already want.Use it: lead with the outcome they are already chasing, then the condition.
    Generation effectPeople retain and engage more with what they produce themselves. Make them guess, solve, or fill in the gap.Use it: pose a guess, a quiz, a "which one" before you answer it.
    Raising the stakesJob: escalationLosses register faster and harder than gains.
    Negativity biasThreats, mistakes, and problems get processed before benefits do.Use it: frame the opener as a mistake they are making, a danger, or a problem, not a benefit.
    Loss aversionThe cost of inaction weighs more than the gain of action.Use it: show what doing nothing is costing them right now, in specific units.
    Emotional arousalHigh-arousal states (fear, urgency, anger, awe) hold attention and drive sharing.Use it: start where the emotion is already at its peak, not where the story chronologically begins.
    Withholding informationJob: retentionAn unfinished thought is hard to walk away from.
    Curiosity gapGive enough to raise a question but not the answer. The gap between what they know and want to know pulls them forward.Use it: imply a payoff, hide the content of it.
    Information gapReveal that there is something they should know and do not. Different from curiosity: this one stings.Use it: "X years doing this and I only just learned…" framing. Make ignorance feel costly.
    Open loopStart something and deliberately leave it unresolved, then close it later.Use it: promise a reveal early, pay it off late; nested loops for longer runtimes.
    Breaking the predictionJob: the turnAttention spikes when a guess turns out wrong.
    Pattern interruptSomething visually or behaviorally wrong for the context. The brain stops to reconcile it.Use it: open on the action that should not be happening.
    Prediction errorContradict what they expect to be true. Error signals force re-processing.Use it: state the counterintuitive result first, explain second.
    NoveltyThe familiar shown from an unseen angle. New information about an old thing.Use it: inside, underneath, slow-motion, cross-section, the step nobody films.
    Von Restorff effectOne element that breaks from everything around it gets remembered.Use it: make the product, the claim, or the person the single outlier in the frame.
    Making it believableJob: proofProof lowers the cost of believing.
    SpecificityNumbers, timeframes, and named details read as true; vague claims read as marketing.Use it: swap every adjective for a figure or a date.
    Social proofEvidence that many others already act this way.Use it: lead with the crowd, explain afterwards.
    AuthorityA credible source raises acceptance and, when unexpected, raises interest.Use it: lead with the credential when it adds intrigue, not just credibility.
    ContrastTwo extremes side by side make the difference legible instantly.Use it: pair the cheap and the expensive, the before and the after, the old way and the new.
    Cognitive fluencyEasy to process feels true. Complexity feels suspicious.Use it: one metaphor, one beat, no sub-clauses.

    Working rules

    Stack two families in the opener, never four. Self-relevance plus one other (stakes, open loop, or prediction error) is the most common winning pair. Three or more reads as a listicle of tricks.

    Hook tests should change the family, not the wording. Two curiosity-gap hooks with different phrasing test copy. A curiosity-gap hook against a loss-aversion hook tests what this audience responds to, which is the information that transfers to the next ad.

    Re-hooks are where long form is won. The Nuora teardown showed the failed-solutions block landing on the midpoint drop-off cliff. Whatever arc you pick, mark the two scenes just before the mechanism and just before the product, and make sure a withholding or prediction-break hook is doing work there.

    How to use this with the Story arc → Story block system

    Pick a spine, then borrow blocks. The mechanism-led arcs are containers: Nuora runs Discovery Story as the spine and drops Unique Mechanism scenes 3–5 and Failed-Solution Reframe scenes 3–5 inside it. The WINPRO script runs Authority Confession with Us vs. Them scenes 2–4 in the middle. Almost every long-form winner composes this way.

    DecisionWhat it changes
    Awareness stageUnaware → AIDA, Symptom Stack, Storytime. Problem-aware → PAS, Discovery, Unique Mechanism. Solution-aware → Failed-Solution, Us vs. Them, Comparison. Product-aware → Review, Demo, Objection Cascade, 4Ps.
    DurationUnder 60s: 3–5 scene arcs only. 1–3 min: 5–8 scenes. 3–5 min: 8–11 scenes, mechanism-led family. 5 min+: Full VSL or Objection Cascade with n expanded.
    Product entryCompact and UGC arcs put the product in scenes 1–3. Mechanism-led arcs hold it to the last quarter. Moving the entry earlier in a mechanism arc is the most common way these ads get broken in editing.
    NarratorDiscovery Story and Storytime need a sufferer. Authority Confession, Investigation and Unique Mechanism work with an expert or a sufferer-turned-researcher. Us vs. Them and Comparison work with either.
