Foundations

General guidelines

These are the non-negotiables that apply to every Nira concept, regardless of format or angle.

1

Write to the exhausted, skeptical center

The reader has been to the vet 3+ times, has a "graveyard of half-finished supplement bottles," and is sleep-deprived. Lead with empathetic recognition in the first 3–7 seconds, explain the mechanism so she feels like the smartest person in the room, then close on identity ("you're the parent who didn't give up"). Lead emotional → support logical → close emotional.

2

Plain language operationally, high emotion emotionally

6th-grade reading level on the surface. Sentence fragments. Present tense. Save jargon (microbiome, dysbiosis, IL-31) for one credibility beat, wrapped in plain words. The avatar is emotionally sophisticated but wants to be talked to like a friend, not marketed to.

3

Always acknowledge the failure history

Every cold concept should nod to "you've tried everything" — the food trials, the shampoos, the other probiotics, the meds. Naming the journey earns the right to make a claim. Frame past failures as not her fault (the products were incomplete), never as her mistake.

4

One avatar + one pain per ad — and match it to its landing page

David's explicit ask: don't scatter. Don't run a "loose stool" ad to an "itching" landing page. Pick the sub-avatar, pick the pain, carry it end-to-end. Tag every concept with its avatar so we can read the data cleanly.

5

Sell the mechanism, not the category

The avatar believes "all probiotic chews are the same." Beat that with the 5-in-1 story and side-by-side specificity. ~60% of our mix should be mechanism-led, ~20% identity/emotional, ~10% new-information/anti-establishment, ~10% direct-response/social proof.

6

Compliance is a creative constraint, not an afterthought

Use "supports," "helps maintain," "promotes a healthy/normal [histamine/immune] response," "targets occasional itching." Never "cures," "eliminates," "stops the itch," "treats," or disease names. Never directly attack Apoquel/Cytopoint — reference the fear obliquely and let the owner's own knowledge do the work. David approves all claims pre-launch.

7

Set the timeline honestly

Never promise days. The believed working window is 8–12 weeks (subtle changes 3–5 weeks, noticeable 6–8, full ~90 days). Honest timelines pre-empt the #1 refund driver: quitting early. This also justifies the Buy-2-Get-1-Free (a full 90-day supply).

8

Realism is the #1 quality bar

David's biggest complaint about AI creative: fake-looking people, fake voiceovers, weak cartoons. Every AI asset must clear a "could this be real?" check. Real homes, real dogs, mixed breeds — never glossy pharma-style studio shots.

The Reader

Avatar & detailed sub-avatars

Core avatar: "The Itchy Dog Owner" — described in the research as "the most desperate buyer in the entire pet supplement category, and the most loyal once a brand actually delivers." 30–65 (sweet spot 35–55), ~70% female, $55K–$180K household, US-only, elevated in the high-allergy South/Texas and damp PNW. High-allergy breeds: French Bulldog, Bulldog, Boston Terrier, Pit mix, Lab, Golden, Boxer, Doodle, often a rescue, 2–7 yrs old.

Where she is when our ad finds her

3+ vet visits. Prescribed Apoquel and/or Cytopoint. Has spent $1,000–$2,500+ (sometimes $10K) on shampoos, sprays, food trials, other probiotics, meds — and most of it hasn't worked. She is exhausted, sleep-deprived, financially drained, guilt-ridden, and skeptical. But she is still searching at 2 a.m. — and that's the moment we reach her.

Primary itch sub-avatars (write each ad to ONE)

A · The "I've Tried Everything" Owner

The broad center. Practical, persistent, burned. Subscribes to remove decision fatigue. "I have a graveyard of half-empty supplement bottles in my pantry. Probably $400 worth, all useless."

B · The Allergy-Med User (Apoquel/Cytopoint)

~Half the avatar. Used the drugs as a last resort; they stopped working or scared her. Knows the FDA cancer warning verbatim. Ready to spend — but wants a root-cause answer, not another suppressant. "I read the label. The cancer warning. I just sat in the parking lot and cried."

C · The Holistic Researcher

Reads studies, ACV rinses, raw/fresh feeders (Farmer's Dog, Open Farm). Already pre-converted to the gut-skin axis. Meaningful male presence. Trusts Dr. Karen Becker / The Forever Dog. Reward specificity and ingredient transparency.

D · The Millennial/Gen-Z Dog Parent

Pet = child. Urban. Researches on Reddit before buying, peer reviews over ads, subscription-comfortable, wants it Instagrammable. "Tucker is literally my baby. I want him to be his happy self again."

Calming sub-avatars (for the secondary product)

Separation anxiety · leash-pullers · behavior issues · dogs in training · over-reactive dogs. Each is its own ad.

The shared identity through-line (the close)

All sub-avatars share four things: (1) total devotion to the dog — never patronize it; (2) lost trust in something (a vet, a brand, a drug); (3) a desire to be "the kind of dog parent who figured it out" (self-respect, not status); (4) they want their relationship with the dog back — "not a chew, not a probiotic — the dog they remember."
Targeting

Who is — and isn't — the customer

✓ Who IS the customer

  • US dog owners whose dog has chronic itching / allergy symptoms that haven't resolved.
  • Owners who've already tried meds, food trials, other supplements — and are disillusioned but still motivated.
  • People who prioritize their dog's health and treat the dog as family; willing to pay premium for a real fix.
  • Allergy-med users wary of long-term Apoquel/Cytopoint and seeking a root-cause, natural-leaning option.
  • Subscription-comfortable buyers who want a multi-month supply once something works.

✗ Who is NOT the customer

  • Bargain-hunters — price-shoppers who churn. Never compete on price.
  • Chargeback / serial-return-prone buyers.
  • People who don't prioritize their dog's health or won't act on a visible problem.
  • Anyone outside the USA — only market served.
  • (Tonally) people who want a quick overnight "cure" — set honest expectations to filter them out pre-purchase.
Implication for copy: Don't lead with discounts or "cheapest" framing — it attracts exactly the churny, refund-prone buyer David wants to avoid and erodes the premium, root-cause positioning. Lead with value, certainty, and the guarantee instead.
The Differentiator

Unique mechanisms

Master mechanism — the Gut-Skin Axis

The reframe that powers everything: "Your dog's skin problem isn't a skin problem. It's a gut problem showing up on the skin." ~70–80% of the immune system lives in the gut; when the microbiome is out of balance and the gut lining is "leaky," the immune system overreacts and the itching shows up on the skin. This moves the conversation from "what is my dog allergic to" → "why is my dog's body so reactive in the first place."

Ad structures

Cold: Symptom → Problem (gut-skin) → Solution (5-in-1) → Product → Offer (dark side first 3–5 sec, then bright). Warm: Problem → Solution → Product → Offer. Retargeting/hot: Bright side → Product → CTA. Identity: Identification → Product → Offer.

Creative mix: ~70% UGC / 25% pro / 5% motion · 60% female / 30% male / 10% couples · 90% real-world / 10% studio · serious-warm default with light self-deprecating humor about the failed-solutions journey (never humor at the dog's expense).

Red Light

Angles we should avoid

Hard compliance no-gos (account/page-ban risk — David's explicit priority) Don't use the aggressive language that lives on the brand's advertorials. In Meta ad creative specifically, avoid: "cures," "eliminates," "stops the itch," "guaranteed to stop the itch," "kills parasites," disease names, "FDA-approved," before/after that implies a medical cure, and any direct attack on Apoquel/Cytopoint by name. The "parasites in the gut" claim on the listicle is not ad-safe — keep it on the landing page, not in the ad.

Tone / voice misses

avoid Bright-only emotion that skips the pain. avoid "Fur-baby," "wellness journey," "synergy," "holistic," "optimal," "premium," "clinically formulated" (without proof). avoid Jargon dumps (immunomodulatory, antigen presentation). avoid Calling it "just a dog."

Strategy misses

avoid Price/discount-led hooks (attracts churny, refund-prone buyers). avoid Multi-pain "kitchen sink" ads — one avatar, one pain. avoid Mismatched ad↔landing page (no "loose stool" ad → "itching" page). avoid Promising results in days. avoid Glossy pharma-style studio dogs.

Half-paraphrased VOC reads fake. Use real customer phrasing verbatim, or rewrite entirely. Don't lightly reword a quote — it lands as inauthentic, which is the avatar's #1 trust trigger.

Platform Compliance

Meta ad policy — what we can say & what gets the brand banned

Health/supplement is the single most-restricted category on Meta, and enforcement tightened hard in 2026 (broader healthcare-advertiser verification, retroactive audits of live campaigns, and holistic review that reads the ad copy, the creative, the comments and the landing page together). A pet-supplement brand with aggressive advertorials is squarely in the high-risk zone — this section is how we keep David's pages and ad account alive.

The single biggest risk for Nira: landing-page mismatch. Meta now rejects (and bans) at the ad level when a compliant ad points to a non-compliant page. Nira's advertorials say "parasites," "eliminate," "cured," and "guaranteed to stop the itch." If we run ads to those pages, a clean ad can still get killed — and repeat offenses escalate to page/account restriction. Flag any concept whose destination page carries hard claims; route to the softest compliant page or push David to soften the page.

Operating rules for the team

1

Soften the claim, keep the emotion

Lead with empathy and mechanism, not medical promises. "It's the gut" is a compliant idea; "it cures the allergy" is not.

2

Reframe every "you + condition" line into a third-person observation

"Is your dog itching non-stop?" → "When a dog can't stop scratching, the gut is often the real reason."

3

Check the destination page before launch

The ad inherits the page's risk. If the page makes hard claims, either route elsewhere or flag it — don't assume a clean ad is safe.

4

Substantiate or cut

Every stat, "vet-approved," "clinically proven," and press logo needs backing on file. If we can't prove it, it doesn't go in the ad.

5

David approves all claims pre-launch

Per the MSA, claim accuracy is the client's responsibility; we design to comply. Build the agreed non-compliant word list with him in week 1 and treat it as binding.

Note: Meta's policies are interpreted by automated + human review and change frequently; specifics here reflect mid-2026 guidance and third-party trackers. Treat this as a working safety net, not legal advice — when a claim is borderline, hedge it or route it past David.
