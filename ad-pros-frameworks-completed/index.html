Component 01 — Competitive Alternatives

If Ad Pros didn't exist, what would the operator do instead?

  • 1
    Run media in-house with a junior buyer

    The most common alternative. Operator promotes a marketing manager into a buying role. Cheaper on paper. Loses on creative volume, channel breadth, and senior judgment when accounts get sideways.

  • 2
    A generalist agency on a flat retainer

    Common $5-15K/mo retainer with no kicker, mid-tier strategist, and limited creative output. Indifferent to outcomes. Operator churns out within 9-12 months when growth stalls.

  • 3
    A boutique creative shop plus a freelance media buyer

    Two-vendor stack. Creative shop produces. Buyer runs platforms. No closed loop between what's tested and what's spent. Operator coordinates the gaps.

  • 4
    A fractional CMO or growth advisor

    Senior strategy without execution. $8-20K/mo for advisory, no production capacity, no media operations. Useful but doesn't solve the volume problem.

  • 5
    A rival performance agency (Pilothouse, Common Thread, Disruptive)

    The named-competitor alternatives. Comparable creative + media capability. Mostly flat retainers without true performance kickers. Less proprietary AI infrastructure.

Status quo to beat: "in-house with a junior buyer" — alternative 1 is the dominant default. Most lost deals lose to this, not to another agency.

Play Bigger's POV, completed.

A POV is not a tagline — it's a four-part argument. Frame the problem, name the cost, describe the future, place the company. This is the manifesto skeleton.

01 — Frame the problem

The agency model is structurally misaligned with the operators it serves.

Most performance agencies bill flat retainers regardless of outcome. Their economics reward keeping the account, not winning for the account. When media performance dips, the agency invoice doesn't. When the creative is mediocre, the agency still gets paid. When a brand needs to scale or pivot, the agency optimizes for retention, not transformation. The operator sees an inverted incentive — the agency's worst month is still a profitable month, while the operator's worst month threatens the business. Vendors thrive on misalignment. Operators starve from it.

02 — Name the cost of inaction

The cost of vendor relationships is paid in variance, not fees.

Operators who run misaligned agency relationships don't lose money on the fees themselves. They lose it on the variance the relationship produces — wasted spend on under-tested creative, channel concentration that breaks when the algorithm shifts, retention costs from missed onboarding flows, and the opportunity cost of a year spent rebuilding what should have been built right the first time. A $5M brand running with a flat-retainer agency loses $300K-$800K/year to variance the agency has no economic reason to fix. Multiply that across years. Multiply that across the brands that fold before reaching 9 figures because their growth partner had nothing on the line.

03 — Describe the future

Performance partnerships replace vendor relationships.

The next decade of growth marketing belongs to agencies that price like operators — fees that scale with performance, kickers that compound with spend, and creative networks where everyone in the value chain shares upside. The agency floor becomes minimal; the variable component dominates. Creators stop earning per-asset fees and start earning a share of spend. Brands stop buying retainers and start buying outcomes. The agency that lives this model attracts the operators who think this way, and the operators it attracts make the agency stronger. The flywheel stops being about deliverables and starts being about results.

04 — Place Ad Pros

Ad Pros is the performance partner for 8-figure operators.

Ad Pros runs the structure other agencies have always promised but never built. Variable fees scale with the operator's CPA. The Creator Performance Network shares spend economics with creators. The proprietary intelligence stack — Noor — sees the market 30 days ahead of competitors. The team is built from senior operators, not account managers. The pricing protects the operator on the downside and rewards Ad Pros on the upside. Every part of the model exists to deliver one thing: the next million per month, with shared accountability for whether it lands.

How to use this This is the spine of the manifesto. Expand each section to 400-700 words. Total target: 2,000-3,000 word document published at docs.adpros.com. Reference it from every sales conversation, podcast, and recruiting call. The POV is the substrate everything else gets built on.

Languaging Law — Category Pirates

Yoon's Superconsumers, mapped to Ad Pros.

In every category Yoon studied, ~10% of customers drive 30-70% of profit. Identifying Ad Pros' Superconsumer profile is the single highest-leverage exercise in the framework set.

The scoring inputs

A client is scored on the four signals below. Top 10% by composite score are Superconsumers.

  • Tenure (months) — longer is better; loyalty signals fit
  • Monthly spend ($) — higher signals capacity and trust
  • NPS / qualitative satisfaction — willingness to evangelize
  • Referrals generated (count) — most important signal; Superconsumers recruit other Superconsumers

The Superconsumer profile (hypothesized from named clients)

Based on the case-study set in the 2026 deck (AG1, Kachava, Canada Goose, Support Pets, Audien Hearing, Infiniwell), the common profile is:

  • 1
    Vertical: Health, supplements, pet, or premium DTC tech

    All current named-client wins cluster in these verticals. AG1 (health), Kachava (health), Support Pets (pet), Infiniwell (health), Audien (tech). Suggests product fit for high-AOV recurring-purchase categories with strong story potential.

  • 2
    Spend: $1M+/mo within 6 months of engagement

    Operators willing to scale spend aggressively when the unit economics work. Kachava scaled to $4M/mo managed. Support Pets scaled to $2M/mo. Audien to $2M/mo. Brands that ramp this fast self-select into partnership pricing.

  • 3
    Repeat revenue: 30-60%+

    Infiniwell sustained 60%+ retention at scale. The repeat economics are what make performance media compound. Brands without retention shouldn't be Ad Pros clients — the math doesn't work for either side.

  • 4
    Founder profile: Operator-CFO, not creative founder

    Decision-makers who think in CAC, LTV, payback, and channel mix. Sign quickly when the math works. Reject pitches built on brand vibes. Want forecasts and cohort data, not mood boards.

  • 5
    Channel mix: Multi-platform expansion mindset

    Willing to expand beyond Meta. Kachava expanded to Native (Taboola/Outbrain), TikTok, Pinterest. Audien scaled AppLovin and TikTok Shop. Brands that view channel diversification as a competitive advantage match Ad Pros' capability stack.

  • 6
    Prior agency history: Tried 1-2 before

    They've felt vendor misalignment firsthand. They know what bad looks like. They're predisposed to recognize partnership economics as different. Hardest to convince when they haven't tried anyone before — easiest when they've fired someone.

The exercise to actually run

3-4 hours of work. Pull last 12 months of clients. Score on the four inputs. Identify top 10%. Compare against the hypothesized profile above. Where the data confirms, sharpen the ICP. Where the data contradicts, the hypothesis updates. The output is the new outbound target list and the qualifying questions for every inbound discovery call.

Why this matters more than any other exercise The current outbound likely targets a wider net than the Superconsumer profile. Most agency growth is bottlenecked by chasing the wrong fit. Three-quarters of new client acquisition cost goes to deals that don't close or don't retain. Tightening the ICP to the Superconsumer profile compounds: better deals close faster, retain longer, refer more, and pay more.

Define-Develop-Dominate, applied.

Play Bigger's category lifecycle. Three phases. Knowing which phase determines strategy. The category Ad Pros plays in — performance marketing agencies — is in a different phase than the sub-category Ad Pros wants to claim.

Two parallel lifecycles to track

Lifecycle A: Performance marketing agencies (the broad category)

PHASE 1
Define

Many companies swarm a problem. No clear king. Era of "growth hacking" 2010-2015.

PHASE 2
Develop

Major players emerge: Common Thread Collective, Pilothouse, Disruptive, Trend. 2016-2022.

PHASE 3 — current
Dominate

Mature category with established incumbents and known economics. Hard to enter as a new general-purpose performance agency.

Implication: Ad Pros cannot win by being a better generalist performance agency in a Dominate-phase market. The economics reward incumbents. The strategy is sub-category creation — claim a specific lane within performance agencies that incumbents haven't owned.

Lifecycle B: Performance partnership agencies (the sub-category)

PHASE 1 — current
Define

Many agencies talk about "partnership" loosely. None have systematized CPA-linked variable fees + creator economics + AI ops infrastructure as a coherent category. Ad Pros can anchor the problem.

PHASE 2
Develop

Within 3-5 years: 2-4 named players will fight for the partnership-agency category. Ad Pros' window to be the king is now.

PHASE 3
Dominate

Eventual category king takes ~76% of category economics. Followers compete on price.

Implication: Ad Pros is in Define for partnership agencies. The window to anchor the category in operators' minds is open but won't stay open. The Lightning Strike (Tab 08) is the move that converts Define-phase opportunity into Develop-phase positioning.

The strategic decision Don't compete in the broad category (you'll lose to incumbents). Define and own the sub-category (where the economics still favor early movers). "Performance partner" is the sub-category claim. The next 18 months determine whether Ad Pros becomes the king of it or one of three competitors fighting for it.

The 22 Laws — applied selection.

The Pirates' 22 Laws of Category Design are dense and uneven. Five laws apply directly to Ad Pros. The rest are interesting but secondary. Each below is completed with the Ad Pros-specific answer.

Law 01 — The Different Law

Don't compete on better. Win on different.

Ad Pros' "different": CPA-linked variable fees as the dominant pricing component. CPN with 7/3 economics. Proprietary AI intelligence stack. Senior-only team. Multi-channel breadth at 12 platforms. None of these is "better creative" or "better media buying" — those are benchmark arguments. The differentiation is structural: how the agency is paid, who the agency is staffed with, and what infrastructure the agency runs on.

The trap to avoid: any deck slide or pitch that says "we do X better than other agencies." Replace with "we do X differently — here's the structural reason."

Law 02 — The Niche Down Law

Become known for one thing before you expand.

Ad Pros' niche: 8-figure DTC operators in health, supplements, pet, and premium tech — running $1M+/mo paid spend, 30%+ repeat economics, multi-channel willingness. The entire team, infrastructure, and case studies are tuned for this profile. Brands outside this profile are routed to alternative engagements or declined.

The expansion trap: the urge to take on B2B SaaS, sub-$1M brands, or non-DTC ecommerce because the revenue is real. Each non-fit client dilutes the case-study set, distracts the team, and weakens the position. Discipline now, expand later.

Law 03 — The Languaging Law

The words you choose shape the category.

Ad Pros' language priorities: install "performance partner," "8-figure operator," and "Creator Performance Network" everywhere. Retire "agency," "investment," "Dream Client," "coming soon," "channels included." Every public artifact gets audited and updated within one week — saturation is the point.

The structural follow-through: contracts, MSAs, internal Slack channels, and team job titles also adopt the new language. "Account Manager" becomes "Operator Lead." "Client Success" becomes "Partnership Operations." Languaging works inside the company before it works outside.

Law 04 — The Damming Demand Law

Dam existing demand. Redirect it to your category.

Existing demand to dam: operators currently shopping for "performance agency" or "growth agency" or "media buying agency." That's the demand pool. Redirect it by reframing the search — every Ad Pros artifact, ad, and pitch should reframe the category from "agency" to "partner" within the first sentence.

Dam mechanism: publish content explaining why agency-shopping is the wrong question. "The agency you're considering hiring isn't the right question. The right question is: who's going to share economic accountability with you for the next million per month?" Articles, podcasts, IG content, sales scripts. All redirect the same way.

Law 05 — The 6-10 Law

It takes 6-10 years to design and dominate a category.

Ad Pros' clock: the 6-10 year horizon applies to category creation from scratch. Ad Pros isn't creating a category — it's claiming a sub-category in a market that already exists. The horizon for sub-category dominance is 18-36 months, not 6-10 years. Don't let the long timeline excuse slow execution.

The compressed timeline: 90 days to anchor the position publicly (Lightning Strike). 9 months of steady drip after. 18 months total to be the named "performance partner" in the operator's mind. 36 months to be the default.
