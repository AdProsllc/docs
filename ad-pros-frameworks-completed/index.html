Dunford → the discovery and qualification engine.

Dunford's framework isn't a brand exercise — it's the spine of how Ad Pros qualifies, pitches, and closes deals. Used right, it answers the only question that matters in a sales conversation: why pick us over what you'd otherwise do?

What it's for

Replacing pitch-by-features with pitch-by-alternatives

Most agency pitches lead with services and case studies. Dunford forces a different sequence: name what the prospect would do without you (the alternative), name the capability gap that beats it, name the value the gap creates, name why this prospect specifically cares, name the category the conversation lives in. Every pitch surface — discovery script, deck, proposal, follow-up email — gets rewritten in this order.

How to actually use it

Run the framework once internally, then bake it into every sales artifact

1
Half-day workshop, leadership only

Nehal + Katherine + Dani + sales lead. Block 4 hours. Walk the 5-component sequence on a whiteboard for Ad Pros. Output: one canonical positioning page in Notion or docs.adpros.com. Reference document for everything downstream.

OWNER: Nehal · TIME: 4 hours one-time

2
Rewrite the discovery script around competitive alternatives

Open every discovery call with "what are you doing today for media buying?" and "if you weren't talking to us, what would you do?" — not "tell us about your business." The sales rep listens for which of the five alternatives the prospect names, then matches their pitch to the differentiator that beats that specific alternative.

OWNER: sales lead · TIME: 1 day to rewrite, ongoing in every call

3
Build a four-version deck, one per alternative

Same deck skeleton, four variants. The "vs. in-house" version leads with operational depth and senior-team comparison. The "vs. generalist agency" version leads with the kicker structure and CPN. The "vs. boutique + freelance" version leads with closed-loop integration. The "vs. fractional CMO" version leads with execution capacity. Sales picks the version after discovery.

OWNER: Nehal + design · TIME: 1 week to build, deployed once

4
Add the "best-fit gate" to the lead form

Three qualifying questions on adpros.com inquiry form: monthly revenue, monthly spend, repeat-customer rate. Anything below thresholds gets a different intake flow, not the partnership-pitch flow. The gate enforces the best-fit definition before sales time gets spent.

OWNER: web + sales ops · TIME: 1 day to build

5
Audit every existing sales artifact against the framework

Sales deck, proposal template, contract preamble, follow-up email sequences, case studies. Each one gets a Dunford pass: does it lead with the alternative, name the capability gap, translate to value, address best-fit, anchor in category? Where it doesn't, rewrite.

OWNER: Nehal + Katherine · TIME: 2-3 days

What it produces

One positioning page + four-variant pitch deck + qualifying gate

The canonical positioning page becomes the source of truth for messaging across the company — anyone writing copy, recording video, or pitching reads from it. The four-variant deck lets sales tailor the pitch in real-time based on what the prospect surfaces in discovery. The qualifying gate stops Ad Pros from wasting sales time on sub-Dream-Client prospects.

Cadence

Built once. Audited quarterly. Rewritten when reality shifts.

The framework gets a one-time build. The artifacts that come from it run for a year minimum. Quarterly: review which alternative is winning the most lost deals, sharpen the pitch against it. When a new product launches (Intelligence Stack), when the economy shifts, or when a new competitor enters — rerun the framework end-to-end.

What changes downstream

Discovery questions, proposal language, win-rate diagnostics

Once Dunford is baked in, sales conversations get measurably shorter. Prospects who don't fit the best-fit profile drop off earlier. Proposals stop reading like service menus and start reading like deal structures. Lost-deal post-mortems become useful — instead of "they went cheaper," sales can name which alternative beat Ad Pros and on which dimension. That insight feeds the next quarterly audit.

How this fails

Treating the framework as a brand exercise

The most common mistake: completing the framework, publishing it as a positioning doc, then leaving sales to keep pitching the way they always did. The framework only works if it changes what gets said in real conversations. If Nehal completes the components but Dani's discovery calls still open with "tell us about your business," Dunford stays a document, not an operating system.

POV → the manifesto Ad Pros references everywhere.

The Point of View isn't marketing copy. It's the foundational argument every other communication points back to. Once published, it becomes the citation Nehal references on podcasts, the article sales sends pre-call, the document the team reads on day one of onboarding.

What it's for

One canonical argument that explains why Ad Pros exists

Most agencies have a tagline and a services page. Neither answers "why does this company need to exist?" The POV answers it in 2,500-3,000 words. Once it exists, every shorter communication — IG post, podcast clip, sales pitch, recruiting message — becomes a referenced excerpt of the larger argument. That referencing creates consistency at scale that no individual artifact can.

How to actually use it

Write it once, then point everything to it

1
Draft the manifesto using the four-part skeleton

Frame the problem (agencies are misaligned with operators), name the cost ($300K-$800K per $5M brand annually lost to variance), describe the future (performance partnerships replace vendor relationships), place Ad Pros (the structure other agencies promised but never built). Each section 600-800 words. Total 2,500-3,000.

OWNER: Nehal · TIME: 8-12 hours of writing

2
Publish at docs.adpros.com

Permanent URL. Versioned. Referenced from sales emails, podcast bios, the website footer, recruiting pages, and the deck appendix. Every Ad Pros employee reads it on day one. Every prospect receives it before discovery.

OWNER: Nehal + comms · TIME: 1 day to publish

3
Create five derivatives within 30 days of publishing

One Twitter/X thread (12 posts), one LinkedIn long-form post, one IG carousel (10 slides), one 10-minute podcast monologue, one short loom for the deck appendix. Each derivative is a different format of the same argument — no new claims, just compression.

OWNER: comms + Nehal · TIME: 1 week

4
Wire it into the discovery flow

Every prospect who books a discovery call receives an email with the manifesto link two days before. Sales opens the call with "did you read the manifesto?" — if yes, the conversation jumps ahead by 20 minutes. If no, sales spends the first 5 minutes summarizing the four-part argument before getting into the prospect's situation.

OWNER: sales + ops · TIME: 1 day to set up automation

5
Update once a year

The manifesto is not static. Annual review. New examples replace dated ones. New case studies promoted into the argument. New industry shifts addressed. Versioned (v1, v2) so historical references still work but the current canonical is always current.

OWNER: Nehal · TIME: 1 day annually

What it produces

A 3,000-word document everything points back to

Output is a long-form argument with a stable URL. Cited from podcast bios, sales emails, the website footer, IG bios, recruiting pages. The artifact's value compounds over time — each new derivative reinforces the original, each citation drives traffic back to the canonical. After a year, the manifesto becomes the most-read page on docs.adpros.com.

Cadence

Written once. Referenced daily. Updated annually.

The POV is not a content channel. It's a foundational document. Daily activity references it (every IG post, every sales email). Quarterly activity tests it (does the argument still hold against new market data?). Annually it gets a refresh. The compound effect comes from the reference frequency, not the rewrite frequency.

What changes downstream

Sales velocity, content cadence, recruiting conversion

Pre-read prospects close in 30-50% fewer calls because they walk in agreeing with the premise. Content production gets faster — every post, video, or deck slide can be sourced from the manifesto rather than generated from scratch. Recruiting improves because senior operators self-select; people who agree with the POV are the people Ad Pros wants to hire, and the manifesto tells them whether they fit before they apply.

How this fails

Writing it and forgetting it

The failure mode is treating the POV as a publishing event rather than an operating system. Manifestos that sit on a website without being referenced lose authority within 6 months. The discipline is to make the POV the most-cited internal document in the company — referenced in every Slack debate about positioning, every sales objection-handling guide, every onboarding doc. If Ad Pros employees can't quote three sentences from it after 60 days, the manifesto failed to operationalize.

Magic Triangle → the consistency check before any decision.

The Magic Triangle isn't an exercise — it's a decision filter. Every meaningful Ad Pros decision (new pricing, new hire, new service, new client) gets evaluated against three questions: does this fit the position, does it fit the product offering, does it fit the company. Decisions that fail on any side weaken the position over time even when they look profitable in isolation.

What it's for

Catching position-eroding decisions before they ship

Ad Pros makes 50+ meaningful business decisions per quarter — pricing tweaks, new service offerings, hiring choices, client acceptance, partnership proposals, content angles. Without a filter, each decision optimizes locally and the position drifts globally. The triangle is the filter: it forces every decision through three questions before approval. Decisions that strengthen all three sides get fast-tracked. Decisions that weaken any side need explicit justification.

How to actually use it

Three questions, asked in order, on every meaningful decision

Q1
Does it fit the category? (Position-fit)

Does this decision reinforce "performance partner for 8-figure operators" — or weaken it? A new pricing tier that targets $50K/mo brands fails position-fit even if revenue-positive. A senior hire who's only worked at agencies passes position-fit if they bring partnership experience.

Q2
Does it fit the product? (Product-category fit)

Does this decision reinforce or contradict the existing service architecture? Adding "agency-level SEO" services would fail because it's not a performance-partnership service. Adding "AI breakthrough scoring" passes because it sharpens the existing creative system.

Q3
Does it fit the company? (Company-category fit)

Does this decision require capabilities the current team doesn't have, infrastructure that doesn't exist, or operating models that contradict the current structure? Taking on a $200K/mo retainer client fails if the team can't deliver senior-operator service at that floor. Adding a fractional CMO offering fails if no one on the team has the credibility for it.

What it produces

A simple yes/no/needs-work answer on every decision

Decisions that pass all three sides: ship without further debate. Decisions that fail one side: explicitly accept the cost or redesign the decision until it passes. Decisions that fail two or more sides: reject. The triangle isn't bureaucracy — it's a 90-second mental check that prevents 90% of position-eroding moves before they happen.

Cadence

Applied to every meaningful decision in real-time

Not a quarterly review. Not a strategic planning session. A real-time filter applied in the moment a decision surfaces. Used in: leadership 1:1s ("does this hire pass the triangle?"), pricing discussions ("does this discount break product-fit?"), client-acceptance conversations ("is this client worth the position drag?"), partnership pitches ("does this co-marketing arrangement reinforce the category?"). The triangle's value comes from frequency of use, not depth of analysis.

What changes downstream

Strategic discipline replaces revenue opportunism

Once the triangle is the standard, Ad Pros stops taking on position-eroding revenue. Sub-Dream-Client deals get politely declined. Service expansions outside the partnership category get rejected. Hires that don't fit the senior-operator profile get filtered out. Each individual "no" looks like a missed opportunity. Compounded across a year, the "no's" are what protect the position from drift.

How this fails

Skipping the filter when the revenue is tempting

The first $50K/mo proposal from a $1M/year brand will tempt the team to skip the filter "just this once." So will the first request to add a service the team has capacity for. The triangle only works as a filter if it's applied especially to the tempting cases — those are the decisions that drift the position over time. The discipline is to apply it more rigidly when the revenue argument is strongest.

Languaging → the ongoing word audit.

Languaging is not a one-time rename. It's a continuous discipline of replacing vendor language with partner language across every artifact, every communication, every piece of internal copy — and catching drift when it sneaks back in.

What it's for

Saturating the market with three names until they become category

The market doesn't believe a positioning until it hears the same words from multiple directions repeatedly. Ad Pros has three names to install: "performance partner" (what Ad Pros is), "8-figure operator" (who Ad Pros serves), "Creator Performance Network" (the category-claim asset). Languaging is the discipline of getting these three names into every public surface, every contract, every podcast appearance, every internal Slack channel — until the market starts using them back.

How to actually use it

One-week saturation sprint, then ongoing word policing

1
Run the one-week saturation sprint

Block one full week. Audit and replace across every public surface: adpros.com homepage, sales deck, proposal template, contract preamble, IG bios (Nehal + Ad Pros), LinkedIn company page, email signatures (entire team), Slack channel descriptions, podcast bios, internal job titles. Don't stretch this over months — saturation is the point.

OWNER: Nehal + comms + ops · TIME: 1 week, full team

2
Build the language-replacement reference card

One Notion page or printable card with the retire/install pairs: "investment" → "partnership economics", "Creative Strategy" → "The Breakthrough System", "coming soon" → "live with N brands", "Dream Client" → "best-fit operator", "channels included" → "all channels covered", "we help brands grow" → "we partner with operators". Every team member has it pinned in Slack and on their desk.

OWNER: Nehal · TIME: 2 hours

3
Add the language pass to every content workflow

Every external artifact — IG post, LinkedIn post, podcast script, blog draft, sales email, deck slide — gets a language pass before publishing. The pass takes 60 seconds: scan for retired terms, check for the three names, fix and ship. This becomes a checkbox in every content review process.

OWNER: anyone publishing externally · TIME: 60 seconds per artifact

4
Train the sales team to use the language live

Sales calls are where the language either propagates or dies. Roleplay the discovery script with the new vocabulary. Record three calls per week, audit them for vendor-language drift, share findings with the team. Within 90 days, every sales rep speaks the new language naturally; the old language sounds wrong even on internal calls.

OWNER: Dani + sales lead · TIME: 1 hour weekly for 12 weeks

5
Catch market echo and amplify it

When prospects, clients, or peers start using the new language back ("we want a performance partner, not another agency") — capture it. Screenshot the message. Share it internally. Use it as proof in future sales conversations. Market echo is the leading indicator that languaging is working.

OWNER: comms · TIME: ongoing

What it produces

Consistent language across every Ad Pros surface

After 90 days: zero vendor-language artifacts public-facing. Every team member uses the three names without prompting. Sales calls open with "performance partner" within the first sentence. Contracts begin with partnership-economics language. The website, the deck, the IG, the podcast bios all reinforce the same three names. Market echo begins appearing in inbound discovery calls.

Cadence

One-week sprint, then continuous policing

Initial sprint compresses the work into one week. After that, languaging becomes a 60-second checkbox on every external artifact and a weekly call audit for sales. Quarterly: full audit of public surfaces to catch drift. Languaging is never "done" — it's a permanent operating discipline like deployment hygiene in engineering.

What changes downstream

Brand recall, market echo, recruit conversion

Within 60 days: prospects reference Ad Pros by the new language in inbound emails. Within 90 days: peer agencies start mimicking the language (validation). Within 6 months: the three names appear in industry articles and podcast intros without Ad Pros prompting them. Recruiting conversion improves because the language attracts senior operators who want to work for a partner-model company, not a vendor agency.

How this fails

Inconsistent application across surfaces

The most common failure: the website gets updated, the IG bios get updated, but the sales deck still says "Total Investment: $24,060/month" and the contract still says "agency services agreement." The market doesn't process partial updates as repositioning — it processes them as marketing language inconsistency. If 80% of surfaces use the new language and 20% don't, market echo never starts. Saturation requires 100%.

Superconsumers → the targeting loop that compounds.

Superconsumers isn't a customer-segmentation exercise — it's a closed loop. Identify Ad Pros' top 10% of clients by composite score. Reverse-engineer their commonality. Filter outbound to that profile only. Watch the next cohort of clients match the profile. Tighten the profile again. Repeat.

What it's for

Stop chasing the wrong fit; double down on the right one

Most agency growth bottlenecks aren't about lead volume — they're about lead-quality variance. Ad Pros likely closes 1 in 10 of inbound and outbound combined. The 9 that don't close cost as much sales time as the 1 that does. Superconsumers is the framework that says: stop targeting all 10, target the profile of the 1 that closed best, retained longest, and referred most. The loop tightens over time as data gets clearer.

How to actually use it

Quarterly scoring exercise that updates outbound targeting

1
Pull the client roster and score it

Every active and recently-churned client. Score on four signals: tenure (months active), monthly spend ($), satisfaction/NPS (1-10), referrals generated (count). Composite score = weighted average. Identify the top 10% by composite score. These are the Superconsumers.

OWNER: Nehal + ops · TIME: 3-4 hours quarterly

2
Profile the Superconsumers

Document common attributes across the top 10%: vertical (health, supplements, pet, tech?), monthly spend tier, repeat rate, founder type (CFO-operator vs creative founder), prior agency history (1, 2, 3+ before Ad Pros), channel mix (Meta-only vs multi-channel), AOV range. Output: a one-page Superconsumer profile that's testable on any new prospect.

OWNER: Nehal · TIME: 2 hours quarterly

3
Update outbound targeting to the profile

Rebuild outbound prospecting around the Superconsumer profile. Apollo lists, LinkedIn searches, partner referral asks, podcast guest pitches — all filtered to brands that match the profile. Sub-profile prospects either get a different (more transactional) intake flow or get declined.

OWNER: sales lead + ops · TIME: 1 day to rebuild

4
Add the profile to inbound qualification

Sales discovery calls include 2-3 questions designed to test profile fit: "what's your repeat rate?" "what does your channel mix look like?" "have you worked with agencies before?" Sales scores fit during the call. Above-threshold prospects get the partnership pitch. Below-threshold get a different conversation.

OWNER: sales · TIME: ongoing in every call

5
Run the scoring loop again next quarter

90 days later: rerun the scoring on the new client roster. Did the new clients match the profile? Did the profile predict retention? Did Superconsumers refer? Tighten the profile based on the new data. Repeat indefinitely.

OWNER: Nehal + ops · TIME: 3-4 hours quarterly

What it produces

A one-page Superconsumer profile + sharper outbound + faster qualification

Tangible artifact: the one-page profile (vertical, spend tier, repeat rate, founder type, prior history, channel mix). Operational outputs: an outbound list filtered to the profile, a qualifying script that tests fit in 5 minutes, a quarterly report comparing actual closed clients against the profile to validate or update it.

Cadence

Quarterly scoring, continuous targeting, annual profile reset

Quarterly: rescore the roster, refresh the profile, update outbound targeting. Continuous: every new lead gets scored against the profile during qualification. Annually: zoom out — does the Superconsumer profile from a year ago still hold, or has the market shifted? Treat the profile as a living document, not a static ICP definition.

What changes downstream

Close rates climb. CAC falls. Retention improves. Referrals compound.

Targeted outbound to a Superconsumer profile typically 2-3x's close rates within two quarters. Sales-cycle length drops 30-50% because qualifying mismatches happens earlier. Retention improves because new clients match the profile of clients who already retained well. Referrals compound because Superconsumers refer Superconsumers — the loop self-reinforces. Combined effect: revenue per sales hour roughly doubles.

How this fails

Sentimentality keeping non-Superconsumers in the funnel

The hardest part isn't the analysis — it's the discipline of declining non-Superconsumer revenue. Founders feel guilty turning down a $1.5M/year brand that's a poor profile fit because the revenue is real. The framework only works if Ad Pros says no to non-fit clients. Each "yes" to a non-Superconsumer client is a quarter of dragged-down retention metrics, diluted case-study fit, and team morale slippage. The loop only tightens if the discipline holds.

Lifecycle → the strategic clock.

Define-Develop-Dominate isn't a model to admire — it's a clock. Knowing which phase a category is in tells Ad Pros what to do, what not to do, and how fast to move. The performance-partnership sub-category is in Define right now, and the window is closing.

What it's for

Knowing whether to move fast or build slowly

The phase determines strategy. In Define phase, the goal is to anchor — claim the language, publish the manifesto, mobilize a Lightning Strike before competitors do. In Develop phase, the goal is to defend — protect the position from challengers, expand the moat, lock in customer relationships. In Dominate phase, the goal is to extend — leverage the king position into adjacent categories. Mistaking the phase wastes a year. Ad Pros' broader category (performance agencies) is in Dominate; the sub-category Ad Pros wants (performance-partnership agencies) is in Define. Strategy must serve the sub-category clock, not the broad-category clock.

How to actually use it

Quarterly phase check, then strategy adjustment

1
Quarterly: name the current phase

One-page review every quarter. Where is the broad performance-agency category? Where is the performance-partnership sub-category? What evidence supports the phase call (number of named competitors, market awareness, search volume on the language)? Phase calls update the strategic posture for the next 90 days.

OWNER: Nehal · TIME: 1 hour quarterly

2
Match strategy to phase

Define phase (current): mobilize, anchor language, publish manifestos, run Lightning Strikes, claim the term. Develop phase: defend the term, expand the moat, lock in client relationships, build ecosystem partnerships. Dominate phase: extend into adjacent categories (e.g., Ad Pros could eventually extend "performance partner" from media buying into adjacent operator services).

OWNER: Nehal · TIME: ongoing

3
Watch for competitor phase shifts

If a rival agency starts publishing manifestos using "performance partner" language, the sub-category just moved from Define toward Develop. The window narrows. Ad Pros must accelerate. Watching competitor signals — their language, their content, their case studies — is part of phase monitoring.

OWNER: comms + Nehal · TIME: ongoing monitoring

4
Set phase-specific milestones

Define-phase milestones (next 12 months): publish the manifesto, run the Q3 Lightning Strike, secure 5+ named clients explicitly using "performance partner" language in their contracts, get the term mentioned in 10+ external publications without prompting. Develop-phase milestones (months 12-24): defend the term against challengers, expand to a second use case, build partner ecosystem. Each milestone is a phase-transition signal.

OWNER: Nehal · TIME: 1 hour to set, ongoing to track

What it produces

A current-phase call + matched strategic posture + transition milestones

Quarterly artifact: a one-page phase report with the current phase call, supporting evidence, strategic implications, and milestones for transition. Used in leadership planning to determine what the next 90 days should prioritize. Prevents the company from defending a position that hasn't been claimed yet (premature Develop) or anchoring a category that's already saturated (delayed Define).

Cadence

Quarterly check, with continuous monitoring of competitor moves

The phase doesn't shift weekly. Quarterly review is enough for the call. But competitor monitoring is continuous — any month a rival publishes positioning content using overlapping language, the phase clock might tick forward. Treat it like watching weather: most days nothing changes, but the days something shifts demand fast response.

What changes downstream

Resource allocation, content cadence, partnership strategy

In Define phase: heavy investment in content, manifestos, podcast tours, public-facing thought leadership. Less investment in formal partnerships or ecosystem-building (premature). In Develop phase: cut content cadence, increase partnership and ecosystem investment, focus on lock-in. The phase determines where the marketing budget goes, what the content team prioritizes, and what types of strategic relationships Ad Pros pursues.

How this fails

Treating the broad category and sub-category clocks as the same

Most agencies look at the broad category, see Dominate, and conclude there's no opportunity. They miss the sub-category in Define. Or they look at the sub-category, see Define, and conclude they have years to act. They miss that the sub-category window typically closes in 18-36 months once a credible challenger anchors language. The discipline is to track both clocks separately and let the sub-category clock drive Ad Pros' near-term strategy.

The 5 Laws → the daily decision tests.

Of the 22 Laws of Category Design, five apply directly to Ad Pros. Each one is a test, not a rule — applied to small decisions every day, not just to strategic ones. The compounding effect of consistent test application is what builds category authority over months.

What they're for

Five tests that prevent slow position drift

The Magic Triangle catches major decisions before they ship. The 5 Laws catch micro-decisions — the IG post tone, the email signature copy, the proposal phrasing, the case-study angle. Individually each micro-decision feels too small to test. Compounded over a year, the micro-decisions either reinforce or erode the position. The 5 Laws are quick mental checks that keep the micro-decisions aligned.

How to actually use them

Five tests, applied to copy, content, and conversations

Law 01
Different Law

Test: Does this copy/pitch/post claim "better than" or "different from"? If "better than," rewrite. Better is a benchmark argument that puts Ad Pros on a treadmill. Different is structural — kicker pricing, CPN economics, AI infrastructure.

Law 02
Niche Down Law

Test: Does this decision broaden Ad Pros' service offering or sharpen its niche? Broadening fails. Sharpening passes. New B2B service expansion → fails. Better health/supplement creative tooling → passes.

Law 03
Languaging Law

Test: Does this use the three names (performance partner, 8-figure operator, CPN)? If not, why not? If using vendor language, rewrite. Every external artifact gets this 60-second check.

Law 04
Damming Demand Law

Test: Does this artifact reframe the prospect's question (from "agency-shopping" to "partner-shopping")? Or does it just answer the question they already had? The first reinforces category. The second feeds the broad category.

Law 05
6-10 Law (compressed)

Test: Does this decision treat the timeline as 18-36 months (sub-category) or 6-10 years (full category)? Long-timeline thinking excuses slow execution. Short-timeline thinking forces urgency. Default to short.

What they produce

Consistent micro-decisions that compound into category authority

No single law application produces visible output. The compound effect is the artifact: after 6 months of consistent law application across every IG post, every email, every deck slide, every internal Slack message, every podcast appearance — the position becomes consistent across surfaces in a way that no single decision could have produced. Market echo follows.

Cadence

Applied to every external artifact and every meaningful internal decision

Daily, in micro. The team writes copy → applies the Different Law and the Languaging Law. Sales pitches a prospect → applies the Damming Demand Law and the 6-10 Law. Leadership debates a service expansion → applies the Niche Down Law. The laws don't slow decisions — applied at speed, they take 30 seconds each. The discipline is making them habitual.

What changes downstream

Tone consistency, decision speed, team alignment

Within 90 days of applying the laws consistently: every public artifact has the same tone and language. Sales pitches sound coherent across reps. Internal debates resolve faster because the team has shared decision criteria. New hires absorb the laws within their first month and apply them naturally — making the laws scale beyond Nehal's individual oversight.

How this fails

Treating the laws as theory rather than tests

Reading the laws and not applying them. Most teams nod at "be different, not better" and then go right back to writing "the best agency for performance marketing" copy. The laws only work if they become reflex — applied without thinking, in the moment of writing or deciding. The discipline is to operationalize them as 30-second mental checks, not as strategic principles to revisit quarterly.

Lightning Strike → the quarterly mobilization.

A Lightning Strike is not a launch event. It's a project-management discipline that compresses 12 months of market education into a single 2-3 week window. Ad Pros runs one in Q3 to anchor "performance partner for 8-figure operators" — and then runs smaller versions every 2-3 quarters to maintain momentum.

What it's for

Concentrating market attention into a window the market can't ignore

Steady-drip content takes 12-18 months to build market recognition. Most repositioning efforts fail because they drip without ever spiking. The Lightning Strike fixes this by concentrating outputs — manifesto, demo, case studies, podcast tour, partner amplification — into a single window where every signal hits the same operators in the same week. The market processes a strike as "this is real," whereas it processes a drip as "this is marketing."

How to actually use it

One major strike per year, supported by mini-strikes quarterly

1
Pick the anchor and the window

Annual major strike: one big anchor (this year: public launch of "The Ad Pros Intelligence Stack") in a specific 2-3 week window (Q3, July). Pick early. Lock the window. Build everything against that date.

OWNER: Nehal · TIME: 1 day decision

2
Build the asset stack 8-12 weeks before the window

Manifesto draft, Intelligence Stack landing page, 5-7 podcast pre-records, 3-5 expanded case studies, IG/LinkedIn content plan, sales script update, partner activation list. Everything ready to publish, none of it published yet. The hardest part is resisting the urge to launch pieces early as they're completed.

OWNER: comms + Nehal · TIME: 8-12 weeks of build

3
Pre-brief the network 1-2 weeks before

Existing clients, peer agencies, technology partners, podcast hosts, press contacts. Tell them the strike is coming, when it lands, what they'll see. Ask for amplification on launch day. The pre-brief turns passive observers into active amplifiers when the window opens.

OWNER: Nehal + comms · TIME: 1-2 weeks of outreach

4
Open the window: launch everything in the first 5 days

Day 1: manifesto + Intelligence Stack landing page live. Day 2: first podcast drops + first case study published. Day 3-5: remaining podcast drops, remaining case studies, partner amplification, daily IG/LinkedIn cadence at maximum frequency. The first week is the highest-density signal window of the year for Ad Pros.

OWNER: full team · TIME: 5 high-intensity days

5
Ride the wave for week 2-3, then resume steady drip

Week 2-3: respond to inbound, amplify market echo, publish second-wave content (rebuttals, follow-ups, expanded analysis). End of week 3: window closes. Resume the steady-drip cadence. The strike isn't permanent — it's a 21-day burst that resets the market's perception of Ad Pros and gives the steady-drip work something to anchor against.

OWNER: comms · TIME: 2 weeks of sustained activation

6
Run mini-strikes quarterly

Smaller versions every quarter — a major case study with named numbers, a notable client win, a new feature in the Intelligence Stack, a podcast tour around a single theme. Mini-strikes maintain momentum between annual strikes. Same project-management discipline at smaller scale.

OWNER: Nehal + comms · TIME: 1 week per mini-strike

What it produces

Concentrated market attention + measurable category claim

Tangible artifacts during the window: manifesto live, Intelligence Stack landing page, 5+ podcast appearances, 3+ named-client case studies, daily social cadence, partner amplification posts. Measurable outcomes within 60 days: inbound discovery calls referencing "performance partner" language, market echo from peers and prospects, recruit pipeline filling with senior operators, named press mentions of the new positioning.

Cadence

One major strike per year, mini-strikes quarterly between

The annual cadence is what builds long-term category authority. The quarterly mini-strikes prevent decay between annual events. Year 1: major strike anchors the position (Q3 2026). Year 2: major strike defends and expands (Q3 2027). Year 3+: major strike extends into adjacent positioning. Mini-strikes fill the gaps with smaller, theme-driven mobilizations.

What changes downstream

Inbound velocity, recruit conversion, peer perception

Within 60 days of a major strike: inbound discovery call volume typically spikes 2-4x for 8-12 weeks before normalizing at a new baseline. Recruit pipeline fills with people who saw the strike content. Peer-agency perception shifts — from "another performance agency" to "the performance-partner agency." The strike doesn't permanently fix any of these; it resets the baseline at a higher level, which the steady-drip work then maintains.

How this fails

Spreading the launches across months instead of compressing into weeks

The most common failure: the manifesto launches in May, the Intelligence Stack in June, the podcast tour in August, the case studies in October. There's no strike — there's a slow drip the market doesn't notice. Compression is the entire mechanism. If the team isn't disciplined about holding completed assets until the window opens, the strike becomes a non-event. The discipline is to delay individual launches even when the work is ready, in service of the concentrated impact.
