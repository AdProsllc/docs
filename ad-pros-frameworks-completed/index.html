Play Bigger's POV — The Manifesto Skeleton

OwnerNehal (writer)
Deliverable2,000-3,000 word manifesto
Time2 weeks drafting + review
Where it livesdocs.adpros.com

A POV is not a tagline. It's a four-part argument: frame the problem, name the cost, describe the future, place the company. This is the manifesto skeleton — expand each section to 400-700 words.

01 — Frame the problem

The agency model is structurally misaligned with the operators it serves.

Most performance agencies bill flat retainers regardless of outcome. Their economics reward keeping the account, not winning for the account. When media performance dips, the agency invoice doesn't. When the creative is mediocre, the agency still gets paid. When a brand needs to scale or pivot, the agency optimizes for retention, not transformation. The operator sees an inverted incentive — the agency's worst month is still a profitable month, while the operator's worst month threatens the business. Vendors thrive on misalignment. Operators starve from it.

02 — Name the cost of inaction

The cost of vendor relationships is paid in variance, not fees.

Operators who run misaligned agency relationships don't lose money on the fees themselves. They lose it on the variance the relationship produces — wasted spend on under-tested creative, channel concentration that breaks when the algorithm shifts, retention costs from missed onboarding flows, and the opportunity cost of a year spent rebuilding what should have been built right the first time. A $5M brand running with a flat-retainer agency loses $300K-$800K/year to variance the agency has no economic reason to fix. Multiply that across years. Multiply that across the brands that fold before reaching 9 figures because their growth partner had nothing on the line.

03 — Describe the future

Performance partnerships replace vendor relationships.

The next decade of growth marketing belongs to agencies that price like operators — fees that scale with performance, kickers that compound with spend, and creative networks where everyone in the value chain shares upside. The agency floor becomes minimal; the variable component dominates. Creators stop earning per-asset fees and start earning a share of spend. Brands stop buying retainers and start buying outcomes. The agency that lives this model attracts operators who think this way, and the operators it attracts make the agency stronger. The flywheel stops being about deliverables and starts being about results.

04 — Place Ad Pros

Ad Pros is the performance partner for 8-figure operators.

Ad Pros runs the structure other agencies have always promised but never built. Variable fees scale with the operator's CPA. The Creator Performance Network shares spend economics with creators. The proprietary intelligence stack — Noor — sees the market 30 days ahead of competitors. The team is built from senior operators, not account managers. The pricing protects the operator on the downside and rewards Ad Pros on the upside. Every part of the model exists to deliver one thing: the next million per month, with shared accountability for whether it lands.

Don't compete on better. Win on different.

Law 02 — The Niche Down Law

Become known for one thing before you expand.

Ad Pros' niche: 8-figure DTC operators in health, supplements, pet, and premium tech — running $1M+/mo paid spend, 30%+ repeat economics, multi-channel willingness. Team, infrastructure, case studies all tuned for this profile. Brands outside it are routed to alternatives or declined.

The words you choose shape the category.

Ad Pros' priorities: install "performance partner," "8-figure operator," "Creator Performance Network" everywhere. Retire "agency," "investment," "Dream Client," "coming soon," "channels included." Every public artifact audited and updated within one week — saturation is the point.

Dam existing demand. Redirect it to your category.

Demand to dam: operators currently shopping for "performance agency" or "growth agency" or "media buying agency." That's the demand pool. Redirect it by reframing the search — every Ad Pros artifact, ad, and pitch should reframe from "agency" to "partner" within the first sentence.

01 — The 5-Component Positioning Framework

April Dunford • Obviously Awesome (2019)

Position any product or service inside an existing category by working five components in a forced order — alternatives first, category last.

What it is

A diagnostic and decision framework for product positioning. Replaces the traditional "fill-in-the-blank" positioning statement with five forced inputs, each of which constrains the next. Built for B2B tech but applies to any service business with a defined buyer.

The 5 sub-components
  • Competitive Alternatives — what would the customer do if you didn't exist? Includes the status quo (spreadsheets, in-house teams, doing nothing) — not just direct competitors.
  • Differentiated Capabilities — features and capabilities you have that the alternatives don't. Stay on capabilities at this step; don't translate to value yet.
  • Differentiated Value — translate each capability into the business outcome the buyer cares about. Value is only valid relative to a specific differentiated capability.
  • Best-Fit Customers — the specific customers who care most about that value. Define narrowly, not broadly.
  • Market Category — the context that makes the differentiated value obvious. Pick a category where your differentiation pops; avoid creating a new one unless you're at $200M+ revenue.
When to use

When repositioning an existing offering, launching into an established market, fixing a sales process that's losing winnable deals, or onboarding new sales/marketing leadership. This is the default framework for any B2B service repositioning.

When not to use

For genuinely new categories that don't yet exist (LLM tooling pre-2023, electric vehicles pre-2010). Use Play Bigger or Category Pirates for those.

02 — The Point of View (POV)

Play Bigger (2016) • Ramadan, Peterson, Lochhead, Maney

A four-part argument that frames the problem the world hasn't named yet, names the cost of inaction, paints the future, and places your company as the inevitable solution.

What it is

The narrative engine of category design. Not a tagline, not a manifesto in the loose sense — a structured four-part argument that educates the market about a problem before pitching the solution. Salesforce's "No Software" was a POV. Tesla's "sustainable transport" is a POV. The POV becomes the substrate every piece of marketing, sales, recruiting, and PR refers back to.

The 4 sub-components
  • Frame the problem — name a problem the market hasn't named yet, in language the market doesn't have. Put the existing model on the wrong side of the argument.
  • Name the cost of inaction — quantify what's lost by accepting the status quo. Often the cost is not paid in fees but in variance, opportunity, or risk.
  • Describe the future — paint the world after the problem is solved. Don't pitch your product yet — pitch the future state.
  • Place your company — position yourself as the inevitable solution given the future you described. Should feel like a discovery, not an ad.
When to use

Whenever a company needs a public-facing thesis larger than its product description. Most useful at company milestones — major fundraises, category claims, leadership transitions, anniversaries. The POV is the document the entire team can reference for years.

03 — The Magic Triangle

Category Pirates • Lochhead, Yoon, Cole

Three sides — product, company, category — must reinforce each other. If any side drifts, the whole position weakens.

What it is

A fit framework. Most companies optimize one side at a time — build a great product, then "do marketing." The Magic Triangle says all three must be designed together. The product has to fit the category. The company has to be structured to deliver on the category. The category has to pull customers toward the product. The Pirates argue category design is the single point of failure — you can have great product and company design and still lose if the category is wrong, miscommunicated, or undefended.

The 3 sub-components
  • Product Design — the product solves the problem the category needs solved. Goal: product/category fit. Not just product/market fit — product/category fit, where the product reinforces the category claim.
  • Company Design — the business model, team, culture, and infrastructure are engineered for the category. Goal: company/category fit. The right people with the right structure to deliver on the category promise.
  • Category Design — the category itself is created or claimed and developed to pull customers toward the company. Goal: category dominance.
When to use

Before any major strategic move (new service, new pricing, new market, hiring leadership). Run the triangle audit — does the move strengthen or weaken any of the three sides? If it weakens any side, restructure or skip.

04 — Languaging (Frame It, Name It, Claim It)

Category Pirates • Lochhead, Yoon, Cole

The strategic, deliberate use of language to change how people think about a category, a problem, or a company.

What it is

A naming and saturation framework. Languaging argues that whoever names a category controls how customers think about it. Henry Ford called the first car a "horseless carriage" — not a faster horse. Sara Blakely invented "Spanx" — not better shapewear. You "Google" something — you don't search-engine it. The Pirates argue the right language operates at three levels and must saturate every artifact to work.

The 3 sub-operations
  • Frame It — define the problem the category solves in language the market doesn't have yet. The frame puts the existing model on the wrong side of the argument before naming the alternative.
  • Name It — give the category, the customer, or the asset a memorable, defensible, structurally-tied name. Bookable as a Google search. Memorable in conversation. Defensible against copycats.
  • Claim It — saturate the name across every public artifact. Languaging only works at saturation. If the language is in the deck but not the website, contracts, IG, or LinkedIn, it doesn't take.
When to use

After positioning is set. Languaging is the rollout step — once you know what you are (Dunford), what you stand for (POV), and how the three sides fit (Magic Triangle), Languaging is how you put the category in the market's mouth.

05 — Superconsumers

Eddie Yoon • Superconsumers (2016)

In every category, ~10% of customers drive 30-70% of profit. Find them, study them, build everything around them.

What it is

An ICP framework rooted in customer-economics research. Yoon's research at the Cambridge Group across hundreds of categories found that a small fraction of customers — Superconsumers — are not just heavy buyers but emotionally invested, vocal, evangelistic, willing to spend 2-3x more than average customers. Superconsumers fund category creation through their willingness to pay premium prices for differentiation. They also recruit other Superconsumers, which is why finding and serving them compounds faster than expanding a customer base.

The 4 scoring sub-inputs
  • Tenure — how long they've been a customer. Loyalty signals fit.
  • Spend — how much they spend. Capacity and trust signal.
  • NPS / qualitative — how willing they are to advocate. Evangelism signal.
  • Referrals — how many other customers they've brought in. The most important signal — Superconsumers recruit other Superconsumers.
When to use

When sharpening ICP for outbound, when struggling to articulate best-fit customers (Component 4 of Dunford), or when growth is bottlenecked by chasing the wrong fit. Most companies are sized 2-3x larger on the wrong customer base than they should be. Superconsumer analysis exposes the right base.

06 — Define-Develop-Dominate Lifecycle

Play Bigger • Drawing on Paul Geroski's economics research

Categories move through three phases. Knowing your phase determines whether to enter cheap, defend hard, or compete on price.

What it is

A category-clock framework. Drawing on the work of economist Paul Geroski, Play Bigger argues every category moves through three predictable phases. The strategy that wins in one phase loses in the next. Most companies misread the phase they're in and apply the wrong playbook.

The 3 phases
  • Define — many companies swarm a problem; no one owns it; the market hasn't named the category yet. The company that anchors the problem in customers' minds becomes thought leader. Cheapest phase to enter, highest risk if the category never matures.
  • Develop — a king emerges; weaker entrants fold or get acquired. The economics start consolidating around 2-3 named players. Strategy: invest hard in defending the king position or claiming #2.
  • Dominate — the king controls 76% of category economics. Followers compete on price. Most expensive phase to enter; almost always doomed unless you're claiming an adjacent sub-category.
When to use

Before entering or expanding into any market. Track both the broad category and any sub-categories you might claim. The right strategy depends entirely on which phase you're in.

07 — The 22 Laws of Category Design

Category Pirates • The 22 Laws of Category Design (2022)

A codified rulebook for category creation. Most operators only need 4-5 of the 22; the rest are useful as reference.

What it is

A book-length set of laws covering category creation. Each law is a heuristic, not a rule — they sometimes contradict each other depending on context. The discipline is knowing which laws apply to your situation. For repositioning inside an existing category (most operators), 4-5 laws do the work. The remaining 17 are reference material.

The 5 laws that apply most
  • The Different Law — don't compete on better; win on different. Better is a benchmark argument; different is a category argument.
  • The Niche Down Law — become known for one thing before you expand. The expansion temptation kills more category claims than any other failure mode.
  • The Languaging Law — the words you choose shape the category. See Tab 04.
  • The Damming Demand Law — dam existing demand and redirect it to your category. Don't create demand from scratch; capture demand that's currently flowing to the wrong place.
  • The 6-10 Law — it takes 6-10 years to design and dominate a true category. Compresses to 18-36 months for sub-category claims.
When to use

As a decision filter, not a strategy document. Print the laws on a wall. When facing a strategic call (pricing, hiring, new vertical, partnership), check whether the decision violates any law. If it does, restructure or decline.

08 — The Lightning Strike

Play Bigger • Ramadan, Peterson, Lochhead, Maney

A coordinated, time-boxed multi-channel mobilization that compresses 12 months of slow market education into a 2-4 week window.

What it is

A launch mechanism. Most companies leak their positioning into the market gradually — a website update here, a podcast there, a press release whenever convenient. The Lightning Strike argues this is the wrong shape for category claims. To anchor a category in the market's mind, every function (PR, content, sales, customer wins, partnerships, internal team) must fire in the same compressed window. The point is concentrated visibility, not spread reach.

The sub-components of a strike
  • Anchor — one named asset that the strike orbits. A book launch, a product GA, a public manifesto, a major event. Without an anchor, the strike has no center of gravity.
  • Coordinated channels — every function activated simultaneously. Typical: manifesto, landing page, podcast tour, case studies, founder personal channels, sales outbound, partner amplification.
  • Compressed window — 2-4 weeks for category creation; 1-2 weeks for repositioning. The compression is the mechanism — spread launches don't strike.
  • Calendar discipline — Q1-Q2 prep (drafting, building, pre-recording), launch window held to a single block, post-strike steady drip references the strike content for 9-12 months.
When to use

For category claims, major repositioning, product launches at scale, or company milestones (IPO, major fundraise, leadership transitions). The Lightning Strike is what converts months of preparation into a single visibility moment the market can't ignore.

When not to use

For incremental improvements, minor product updates, or routine marketing. Reserved for moves big enough to justify the coordinated cost.

How they fit together

The system in sequence

The eight frameworks aren't independent tools — they form a sequence. Each output feeds the next.

  • Dunford defines the position (alternatives, capabilities, value, best-fit, category).
  • POV turns that position into a public argument (frame, cost, future, placement).
  • Magic Triangle tests whether product, company, and category reinforce the position or break it.
  • Languaging turns the position into the words the market hears (frame, name, claim).
  • Superconsumers identifies who values the position most (the top 10%).
  • Lifecycle determines what speed and tactics to use given category maturity (Define, Develop, or Dominate).
  • 22 Laws is the decision filter — does this move strengthen or violate the position?
  • Lightning Strike is the launch mechanism — concentrated visibility that anchors the position publicly.
The compressed reading order

If a new team member only has time to read one framework, give them Dunford. If they have time for two, add Languaging. For three, add the Lightning Strike. The other five are reference material that becomes useful as decisions get harder.
