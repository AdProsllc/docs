Category Design Frameworks

A working analysis of the major frameworks, then filtered for Ad Pros

Working Analysis

Three schools.
One working answer for Ad Pros.

Category design is not one framework. It is three overlapping schools with different theories of how markets get won — Play Bigger and Category Pirates on category creation, April Dunford on positioning inside existing categories, and the older Ries/Trout/Moore/Christensen substrate everyone builds on. Each gives Ad Pros a different lever. The question is not which is right. The question is which combination wins the next 12 months.

01 The thesis

Positioning vs. category creation is the only argument that matters.

The discipline splits cleanly along one axis. On one side, Dunford argues categories emerge — they are not created by companies, they are recognized by smart operators who position themselves into emerging space. On the other, the Play Bigger / Category Pirates camp argues categories are designed — companies that create them capture 70-80% of the resulting profit pool and "category kings" eat the rest of the market.

Both are right in different conditions. Dunford's data is hard to dismiss: of companies that IPO'd on the Nasdaq, only about 7% were creating a new category at the time of going public. The other 93% were positioned in an underserved existing category. Most companies, most of the time, are not doing category creation. They are doing sharper positioning.

But Lochhead's data is also hard to dismiss: the companies that did design new categories — Salesforce, Uber, Airbnb, Tesla, Netflix — captured outlier returns because they framed the rules of the game before competitors arrived. When category creation works, it pays exponentially. When it fails, it fails completely (you spent years educating a market that never showed up).

"It's much easier to say to a customer, 'We're in this category, but we're for this specific need, or we do things in this different way.' You're leveraging what customers already know. It's way harder to create a new category. But sometimes you have no choice."

April Dunford

For Ad Pros: this is not a category creation moment. Performance marketing agencies exist as a category. The opportunity is sharper positioning inside it — "performance partner for 8-figure operators" — using Dunford's mechanics for the position itself, and borrowing language and identity tactics from the Pirates to make the claim feel categorical even though it isn't.

02 The Dunford framework

Five components, in order.

April Dunford's framework, from Obviously Awesome (2019), is the most operationally useful of the three schools. Five components, sequenced. Each one constrains the next.

1

Competitive alternatives

Not just competitors. What would the customer do if you didn't exist? The status quo is the first alternative to beat — a spreadsheet, an intern, doing nothing. Dunford clusters alternatives into 3-5 groups. The Bugatti example: the real competitor isn't Ferrari, it's a yacht, because the buyer is shopping for status, not transportation.

2

Differentiated capabilities (features / attributes)

Once alternatives are listed, isolate what you have that they don't. Stay on features and capabilities at this step — not value yet. Test each one against the alternatives. If a competitor has it too, it isn't a differentiator. Dunford warns founders not to confuse "we do it better" with "we do it differently." Better is a benchmark argument; different is a category argument.

3

Differentiated value

Translate each capability into the value it delivers. A feature that sounds technical becomes a business outcome the buyer cares about. "Real-time attribution" → "you stop wasting spend on channels that look like they're working but aren't." Value is only meaningful when it's tied back to the differentiated capability that produced it.

4

Best-fit customers

The customers who care a lot about the differentiated value — and pay accordingly. Dunford defines best-fit narrowly. Not "anyone who could buy" but "the customers who will close fastest, churn slowest, expand most, and refer their peers." If you cannot describe the best-fit customer in one sentence, the prior steps are not tight enough.

5

Market category

The context you place yourself in so the buyer instantly understands the differentiated value. Pick a category where your differentiation pops. Avoid creating a new category unless you are at $200M+ revenue with the budget to educate a market — the cost of category creation almost always exceeds the cost of sharper positioning inside an existing one.

The mechanics that matter Dunford's framework is recursive. Each component is only valid relative to the one before it. Differentiated value depends on differentiated capabilities. Differentiated capabilities only differentiate against specific alternatives. Best-fit customers are the people who care about that specific value. Market category is the frame that makes that value obvious. Skip a step or do them out of order and the positioning sounds plausible but doesn't win deals.

What Dunford gets right

  • Forces you to define the status quo as a competitor — most agencies skip this and lose to "we'll do it in-house"
  • Separates capability from value — kills the trap of pitching features no buyer cares about
  • Defines best-fit narrowly — protects against the "we serve everyone" trap that destroys positioning
  • Treats market category as a choice, not a given — the same product can be positioned in three different categories with three different best-fit customers

What Dunford under-weights

  • Identity and language as a lever — her framework treats the position as analytic, not narrative
  • Timing and lightning strikes — Dunford's framework is steady-state; it doesn't tell you how to launch a category claim with force
  • The internal company changes that make a position believable — Pirates' Magic Triangle covers this; Dunford does not
03 The Play Bigger framework

Category king flywheel: POV, lightning strike, ecosystem.

Play Bigger (2016) by Ramadan, Peterson, Lochhead, and Maney introduced the discipline. Its core claim: in a designed category, the king takes 76% of category economics. Its core method: design the category, condition the market, mobilize the company.

The five working tools

Point of View (POV)

The narrative engine

A POV is not a tagline or a marketing claim. It is a four-part argument: (1) frame a problem the world hasn't named yet, (2) explain the cost of not solving it, (3) describe a future where it is solved, (4) position your company as the inevitable solution. The POV educates the market about a problem before pitching the solution. Salesforce's "No Software" was a POV — not a feature claim.

Lightning Strike

The launch mechanism

A coordinated, time-boxed, multi-channel mobilization that anchors the POV in the market's mind in a short window. Usually built around a single event (a conference, a launch, a publication) with PR, content, sales, customer wins, and partnerships all firing simultaneously. The point is to compress what would take 12 months of slow market education into a 2-4 week strike where the entire industry moves at once.

Category-Product-Company fit

The structural prerequisite

Category kings design three things in parallel — the category itself, the product that fits the category, and the company structure that can deliver it. Most companies build a product first, then try to retrofit a category around it. Play Bigger argues this almost always fails because product-market fit is the wrong target; category-product-company fit is.

The Flywheel

The compounding mechanism

Once a category king is established, talent, capital, customers, partners, and press all move toward the king. Each new customer makes the next one easier to win. Each new hire makes the next one easier to recruit. Each piece of press makes the next one easier to earn. The flywheel is what justifies the outlier returns of category kings — the position becomes self-reinforcing.

Define-Develop-Dominate lifecycle

The category clock

Drawing on economist Paul Geroski's work, Play Bigger argues categories move through three phases. Define: many companies swarm a problem, no one owns it; the company that anchors the problem in customers' minds becomes thought leader. Develop: a king emerges; weaker entrants fold or get acquired. Dominate: the king controls 76% of economics. Knowing which phase you're in dictates strategy — entering during Define is cheap and risky; entering during Dominate is expensive and usually doomed.

The Play Bigger thesis in one line Don't try to be better. Be different — and design the category that makes "different" the new "better."
04 Category Pirates frameworks

The Magic Triangle, languaging, and the 22 Laws.

Lochhead, Yoon, and Cole — the Category Pirates — extended Play Bigger with a denser toolkit. The most useful pieces for working operators:

The Magic Triangle

Three sides, equal force

A reformulation of Play Bigger's category-product-company fit, with sharper definitions:

  • Product Design: the product solves the problem the category needs solved. Goal: product/category fit.
  • Company Design: the business model and team are engineered for the problem. Goal: company/category fit.
  • Category Design: the category is created and developed to pull customers in. Goal: category dominance.

The Pirates argue that of the three, category design is the single point of failure. You can have great product and company design and still lose if the category is wrong, miscommunicated, or undefended.

Languaging

The strategic weaponization of words

Languaging is the deliberate use of language to change how people think about a thing. Henry Ford called the first car a "horseless carriage" — not a faster horse. Sara Blakely invented "Spanx" — not better shapewear. You "Google" something — you don't search-engine it. The Pirates argue that whoever names the category controls how customers think about it.

Three operations: Frame It (define the problem the category solves in language the market doesn't have yet), Name It (give the category a memorable, defensible name), Claim It (be the company that owns the name in customers' minds).

Superconsumers

Eddie Yoon's contribution

Drawing on Yoon's research, Superconsumers are the 10% of customers who drive 30-70% of category profit. They are not just heavy buyers — they are emotionally invested, vocal, evangelistic, and willing to spend 2-3x more than average customers. Finding and serving Superconsumers is faster than expanding a customer base; they fund category creation through their willingness to pay premium prices for differentiation.

The 22 Laws of Category Design

The codified rulebook

A book-length set of laws covering category creation. Highlights worth knowing without buying the book:

  • The Different Law: don't compete on better; win on different
  • The 6-10 Law: it takes 6-10 years to design and dominate a category
  • The Niche Down Law: become known for one thing before you expand
  • The Languaging Law: the words you choose shape the category
  • The Damming Demand Law: dam existing demand, redirect it to your new category

The Category Design Toolkit (15 frameworks)

The 2023 expansion

The most recent codification — 15 frameworks covering measurement of category creation vs. competition, the No Ocean Strategy (their rebuttal to Blue Ocean), the Niche Down method, and operational tools for prosecuting the Magic Triangle. The toolkit is dense and uneven; Magic Triangle, Languaging, and Superconsumers are the three frameworks worth knowing cold. The rest are useful as tactical references.

The Pirates' weakness Their framework is built for category creation — companies inventing new spaces. For most operators, including Ad Pros, you're not creating a category. You're claiming a sharper position inside one. The Pirates' tools work in that context but require translation: Magic Triangle becomes "position-product-company fit," Languaging becomes "name your position," Superconsumers becomes "your best-fit customers from Dunford's framework." Don't take the rhetoric as gospel; take the mechanics.
05 Yoon's Superconsumers, applied

Why this one framework matters most for agencies.

Of all the Pirates' frameworks, Superconsumers is the most directly transferable to agency economics. The math is brutal and clarifying.

In every category Yoon studied, roughly 10% of customers drive 30-70% of profit. They are not 2x more valuable than average — they are 5-10x more valuable. They renew. They expand. They refer. They tolerate price increases. They make the rest of the customer base more valuable through word-of-mouth.

For Ad Pros, the Superconsumer is the 8-figure operator who treats spend as a P&L line, not a marketing expense. They:

  • Pay performance-linked fees without flinching because the math is obvious to them
  • Stay 3+ years because switching costs and trust compound
  • Refer their peers because their peers are also 8-figure operators
  • Expand spend with you because growing the channel is more valuable than saving 5% on fees
  • Anchor case studies that recruit the next cohort of operators like them

The strategic move is not to broaden the funnel. It is to identify the existing Ad Pros Superconsumers — by spend, by tenure, by referral activity, by NPS — and reverse-engineer what they have in common. Then build the entire acquisition motion around finding more of them.

"Knowing who you're not for is just as important as knowing who you're for."

Christopher Lochhead
06 The substrate

What everyone built on.

All three modern schools cite the same four foundational sources. Worth knowing the lineage to understand which moves are actually new and which are repackaged.

SourceCore ideaWhat modern category design borrowed
Ries & Trout
Positioning (1981)		 The "law of leadership" — better to be first in a category than to be better. Positioning happens in the customer's mind, not in the product. The entire Play Bigger thesis. The Pirates' "category king takes 76%" is Ries/Trout updated with newer data.
Geoffrey Moore
Crossing the Chasm (1991)		 Technology adoption lifecycle: innovators → early adopters → early majority → late majority → laggards. The chasm sits between early adopters and early majority. Dunford's best-fit customer concept. Play Bigger's category lifecycle (Define-Develop-Dominate) is Moore's curve relabeled.
Clayton Christensen
Jobs-to-be-Done		 Customers don't buy products; they hire products to do a job. Understanding the job done lets you compete across categories. Dunford's competitive alternatives concept (the Bugatti vs. yacht example is a JTBD argument). The Pirates' "frame the problem" step in Languaging.
Kim & Mauborgne
Blue Ocean Strategy (2005)		 Don't compete in red oceans of bloody competition. Find or create blue oceans of uncontested market space. The Pirates explicitly reject "Blue Ocean" in favor of "No Ocean Strategy" — but the underlying move (escape competition through differentiation) is the same.

08 Where they actually disagree

Three real fights worth understanding.

Fight 1: Are categories created or discovered?

Pirates / Play Bigger: created. Companies design categories deliberately and the market follows.

Dunford: discovered. Categories emerge from underlying customer behavior; smart companies recognize them early. Only ~7% of public companies created their category at IPO.

Working answer: both are right depending on category maturity. In genuinely new technology spaces (LLMs in 2023, electric vehicles in 2008) categories are partially created. In mature service markets like agencies, categories are almost entirely discovered. For Ad Pros, Dunford is closer to right.

Fight 2: Better or different?

Pirates: never better. "Better" is a benchmark argument that puts you on a treadmill against incumbents. Always different.

Dunford: different is one form of better. The point is differentiated value the buyer cares about — which is sometimes "we do the same thing meaningfully better."

Working answer: Pirates are right rhetorically, Dunford is right operationally. Frame the position as different (rhetorical lever); deliver value that is also better (operational reality). For Ad Pros: "performance partner" sounds different (kicker, equity-like fees) and is also better (lower variance, higher ROAS through alignment).

Fight 3: Lightning strike or steady drip?

Play Bigger: compress market education into a 2-4 week strike. Force the industry to move at once.

Dunford: repositioning is a 3-9 month process of consistent message reinforcement. No strike required.

Working answer: for category creation, lightning strike. For repositioning, steady drip. Ad Pros is repositioning, but should still build at least one Lightning Strike moment (a published manifesto, a podcast launch, a public case study with named numbers) to anchor the new positioning publicly. Without one strike, the new position will read as a tagline change, not a category claim.

09 Filtered for Ad Pros

What to use, what to skip, what to translate.

The honest answer: no single framework fits Ad Pros cleanly because Ad Pros is repositioning inside an existing category (performance agencies) but trying to claim it categorically (the "performance partner" lane). That's a hybrid move, so the framework has to be hybrid too.

From Dunford — use directly

  • The 5-component sequence. Walk through alternatives → capabilities → value → best-fit → category for Ad Pros. The output is the rigorous backbone of the positioning page, sales deck, and discovery script.
  • Competitive alternatives as status-quo. The biggest alternative isn't another agency — it's the client running media in-house, or with a junior buyer, or with a hands-off agency they've stopped trusting. Beat that, win the deal.
  • Best-fit customer narrowness. 8-figure operator is the right starting frame, but tighten further: 8-figure operator with $300K+ monthly spend, ecommerce DTC, founder-led, has tried 2+ agencies before.
  • Category as a choice. Ad Pros could position inside "performance agency," "growth partner," "fractional CMO," or "media buying firm." The category choice constrains everything downstream. "Performance partner" is the right call because it implies shared outcome, which protects pricing.

From Play Bigger / Pirates — translate, don't transplant

  • POV → Manifesto. Build the full Point of View document for Ad Pros: the problem (agencies are misaligned with operators), the cost (variance, churn, mediocre creative), the future (performance partnerships replace vendor relationships), the inevitable solution (CPA-linked fees, equity-like creator economics, AI ops infrastructure). Publish it. This becomes the substrate for everything else.
  • Magic Triangle → Position-Product-Company fit. Translate: the position (performance partner) must match the product (kicker pricing, Creator Network, AI ops) must match the company (senior operators, BigQuery/Supabase infrastructure, founder-led). All three must reinforce. If any one drifts, the position weakens.
  • Languaging → Naming the position. "Performance partner" is the languaging move. So is "8-figure operator." So is "Creator Performance Network." Each of these is a deliberate name designed to shape how the market thinks about Ad Pros and its clients. Worth auditing every public artifact for whether it reinforces or dilutes these names.
  • Lightning Strike → One anchor moment. Don't try to mobilize a category-king-scale launch. Do build one strike moment in the next 90 days: a published manifesto, a podcast appearance with named clients, a public results dashboard, or a launched book/playbook. The point is to make the new positioning visible at scale once, so the steady-drip work after has something to refer back to.
  • Superconsumers → Map current ones. The clearest, fastest exercise: list current Ad Pros clients by tenure × spend × NPS × referrals. The top 10% are Superconsumers. What do they have in common? That commonality is the new ICP for outbound.

What to skip

  • Category creation rhetoric. Ad Pros is not creating a category. Skip the "we are inventing X" language; it overpromises and undercredibles. Position inside performance agencies as the partnership-model category leader.
  • The 6-10 year horizon. The Pirates' 6-10 Law applies to category creation. For repositioning, 6-12 months is the right horizon. Don't let the long timeline excuse slow execution.
  • The full 22 Laws. Read them, internalize 4-5, ignore the rest. The Different Law, Niche Down Law, and Languaging Law are the three that pay for the price of the book.
  • Blue Ocean / No Ocean debates. Theological, not operational. Skip.
10 A working operating model

The hybrid Ad Pros should run.

If you collapse all of the above into a single working model, it looks like this:

1

Run Dunford's 5 components for Ad Pros end-to-end

Half-day exercise. Output: a single page that names competitive alternatives (in-house, generic agencies, freelance buyers, fractional CMOs), differentiated capabilities (CPA kicker, Creator Network, AI ops, senior team), differentiated value (lower variance, higher ROAS, faster scale), best-fit customer (8-figure DTC operator, $300K+ spend, founder-led), and market category (performance partner). This page becomes the source of truth for everything else.

2

Write the Ad Pros POV manifesto

2,000-4,000 words. Frame the problem operators face, the cost of the status quo, the future where performance partnerships replace vendor relationships, and the role Ad Pros plays in that shift. Publish at docs.adpros.com. This is the document you reference in every sales conversation, podcast appearance, and recruit call.

3

Map current Superconsumers

Pull last 12 months of clients. Score by spend × tenure × NPS × referrals. Identify the top 10%. Document what they have in common. That commonality is the next 12 months of outbound ICP.

4

Audit Languaging across all surfaces

Every public artifact (website, deck, IG, LinkedIn, email signatures, contracts) gets audited for whether it uses or dilutes the three names: "performance partner," "8-figure operator," "Creator Performance Network." Replace generic agency language ("we help brands grow") with the named positioning.

5

Stage one Lightning Strike in Q3

Pick the moment: a published manifesto, a podcast launch, a public results dashboard, or an industry talk with named numbers. Concentrate PR, content, sales outreach, and partner activations into a 2-4 week window around it. The point is to make the new positioning unmistakable to the market once, so the steady-drip work after compounds against a real anchor.

6

Run the steady drip for 9 months

After the strike, weekly cadence: one client win story, one operator-language IG post, one targeted outbound campaign to next-cohort Superconsumers, one internal team training reinforcing the position. Each touch reinforces the same five-component frame. The market starts hearing the same words from multiple directions, which is when positioning stops being a claim and becomes a reputation.

Use Dunford's framework. Borrow Play Bigger's energy. Steal the Pirates' language. Skip the category-creation rhetoric. Build the position deliberately — and back it with one moment that makes it visible.
